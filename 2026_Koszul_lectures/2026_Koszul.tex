\section*{Convention}

Throughout the course, $\Bbbk$ is a field.
All rings are unital and associative.
All modules are right modules unless otherwise specified.
When we discuss dimensions, we mean dimensions as $\Bbbk$-vector spaces.


\section{Lecture 1: Graded algebras, modules, and duals}

\subsection{Algebras and modules}

\begin{definition}
A \defn{$\Bbbk$-algebra} is a $\Bbbk$-vector space with an associative unital multiplication that is \defn{bilinear}, meaning linear in each variable.
\end{definition}

A right $A$-module is understood to be a $\Bbbk$-vector space $M$ with a bilinear action satisfying $(ma)b=m(ab)$ and $m1_A=m$ for $m\in M$ and $a,b\in A$.
A module homomorphism $f:M\to N$ is linear and satisfies $f(ma)=f(m)a$.
A homomorphism of $\Bbbk$-algebras is a linear map preserving multiplication and the identity.

\begin{example}\label{ex:triangular-algebra}
(1) The matrix ring $\Mat_n(\Bbbk)$ is a $\Bbbk$-algebra.
The space of $1\times n$ row vectors is a right $\Mat_n(\Bbbk)$-module by matrix multiplication.

(2) Let $\Bbbk A_n$ be the \defn{upper triangular matrix ring}, which is also a $\Bbbk$-algebra.
\[
\Bbbk A_n = \begin{pmatrix}
\Bbbk & \Bbbk & \cdots & \Bbbk \\
0 & \Bbbk & \cdots & \Bbbk \\
0 & 0 & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & \cdots & 0 & \Bbbk
\end{pmatrix}
\]
For those familiar with path algebras, $\Bbbk A_n$ is the path algebra of a \defn{linearly oriented $A_n$ quiver}, which explains the notation. We use matrix calculations, so no knowledge of quivers is required.
Write $e_{ij}$ for the matrix with $1$ in entry $(i,j)$ and zero elsewhere.
Our convention composes paths from left to right: $e_{ij}$ represents $i\to j$, and $e_{ij}e_{j\ell}=e_{i\ell}$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Categories and functors}\label{subsec:category-language}

We organise modules together with their homomorphisms. A category records both: specifying the objects alone does not specify a category.

\begin{definition}\label{def:category}
A \defn{category} $\CC$ consists of objects, a set $\Hom_{\CC}(X,Y)$ of \defn{morphisms} $f:X\to Y$ for each pair of objects, and composition sending $f:X\to Y$ and $g:Y\to Z$ to $g\circ f:X\to Z$.
Each object $X$ has an identity $\id_X:X\to X$, and
\[
h\circ(g\circ f)=(h\circ g)\circ f,
\qquad \id_Y\circ f=f=f\circ\id_X
\quad\text{for all composable }f,g,h.
\]
\end{definition}

A morphism $f:X\to Y$ is an \defn{isomorphism} if some $g:Y\to X$ satisfies $g\circ f=\id_X$ and $f\circ g=\id_Y$; we then write $X\cong Y$.
A \defn{full subcategory} of $\CC$ consists of chosen objects of $\CC$, with all morphisms between them and the same composition.

\begin{example}\label{ex:module-categories}
The category $\Mod A$ has right $A$-modules as objects; its morphism set $\Hom_A(M,N)$ consists of right $A$-linear maps $M\to N$, with the usual composition.
Its full subcategory $\mod A$ consists of finite-dimensional modules.
In particular, $\Mod\Bbbk$ and $\mod\Bbbk$ are the categories of all and of finite-dimensional vector spaces, respectively, with linear maps.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}\label{def:functor}
A \defn{functor} $F:\CC\to\CD$ assigns an object $F(X)$ to each object $X$ and a morphism $F(f):F(X)\to F(Y)$ to each $f:X\to Y$, such that
\[
F(\id_X)=\id_{F(X)},\qquad F(g\circ f)=F(g)\circ F(f)
\quad\text{for all composable }f,g.
\]
\end{definition}

For example, forgetting the $A$-action gives a functor $\Mod A\to\Mod\Bbbk$: it keeps the underlying vector spaces and linear maps.
The \defn{identity functor} $\id_{\CC}$ leaves every object and morphism unchanged; functors compose by applying one after the other.
Every functor preserves isomorphisms, since $F(f^{-1})$ is inverse to $F(f)$.

The \defn{opposite category} $\CC^\op$ has the same objects as $\CC$, with $\Hom_{\CC^\op}(X,Y)=\Hom_{\CC}(Y,X)$ and composition reversed.
A \defn{contravariant functor} from $\CC$ to $\CD$ means a functor $\CC^\op\to\CD$.
Thus it sends $f:X\to Y$ to $F(f):F(Y)\to F(X)$ and satisfies $F(g\circ f)=F(f)\circ F(g)$.
Duals will provide our main examples.

\begin{definition}\label{def:natural-transformation}
For functors $F,G:\CC\to\CD$, a \defn{natural transformation} $\eta:F\to G$ is a family of morphisms $\eta_X:F(X)\to G(X)$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:naturality-square}
\xymatrix@C=45pt@R=20pt{
F(X) \ar[r]^{\eta_X} \ar[d]_{F(f)} & G(X) \ar[d]^{G(f)}\\
F(Y) \ar[r]_{\eta_Y} & G(Y)
}
\qquad
G(f)\circ\eta_X=\eta_Y\circ F(f)
\quad\text{for every }f:X\to Y.
\end{equation}
If every $\eta_X$ is an isomorphism, $\eta$ is a \defn{natural isomorphism}, written $F\cong G$.
\end{definition}

The square in \eqref{eq:naturality-square} is said to \defn{commute}: its two routes from $F(X)$ to $G(Y)$ give the same morphism.
For example, for fixed $c\in\Bbbk$, the maps $c\id_M:M\to M$ define a natural transformation $\id_{\Mod A}\to\id_{\Mod A}$ because $f(cm)=cf(m)$ for every module homomorphism $f:M\to N$.
It is a natural isomorphism when $c\neq0$.

A functor $F:\CC\to\CD$ is an \defn{equivalence} if there is a functor $G:\CD\to\CC$ with natural isomorphisms $GF\cong\id_{\CC}$ and $FG\cong\id_{\CD}$.
A \defn{duality} between $\CC$ and $\CD$ is an equivalence $\CC^\op\to\CD$.
Thus an equivalence recovers objects and maps up to natural isomorphism; a duality also reverses the direction of maps.

\subsection{Semisimple algebras}

\begin{definition}
A module $L$ is \defn{simple} if $L\neq 0$ and its only submodules are $0$ and $L$.
A module is \defn{semisimple} if it is a direct sum of simple modules.
An algebra $B$ is \defn{semisimple} if it is semisimple as a right module $B_B$ over itself.
The \defn{Jacobson radical} $\rad B$ is the intersection of all maximal right ideals of $B$.
\end{definition}

We use the following standard facts without proof; see \cite[Lecture~2]{Bez23}.
Recall that a \defn{division $\Bbbk$-algebra} is a non-zero $\Bbbk$-algebra whose non-zero elements are invertible.

\begin{theorem}[Artin--Wedderburn]\label{thm:artin-wedderburn}
For a non-zero finite-dimensional $\Bbbk$-algebra $B$,
\[
B\text{ is semisimple}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\rad B=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
B\cong\prod_{i=1}^r\Mat_{n_i}(D_i),
\]
where $r,n_i\geq 1$ and each $D_i$ is a finite-dimensional division $\Bbbk$-algebra.
If $\Bbbk$ is algebraically closed, then $D_i=\Bbbk$ for every $i$.
For semisimple $B$, every left or right $B$-module is semisimple, and every submodule has a complement. In particular, the definition is independent of the chosen side.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:semisimple-summands}
Every finite-dimensional left or right module over a semisimple algebra $S$ is a direct summand of $S^{\oplus n}$ for some $n$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
A finite generating set gives a surjection $S^{\oplus n}\onto M$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:artin-wedderburn}, its kernel has a complement, which maps isomorphically onto $M$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:semisimple-algebras}
The algebras $\Bbbk^{\oplus r}$ and $\Mat_n(\Bbbk)$ are semisimple by Theorem~\ref{thm:artin-wedderburn}.
For $A=\Bbbk A_2$, the ideal $J=\Bbbk e_{12}$ satisfies $J^2=0$.
If $L$ is simple, then $LJ$ is either $0$ or $L$; the latter would give $L=LJ^2=0$. Hence every maximal right ideal contains $J$.
Since $A/J\cong\Bbbk\oplus\Bbbk$, those ideals are $\Bbbk e_{11}\oplus\Bbbk e_{12}$ and $\Bbbk e_{12}\oplus\Bbbk e_{22}$. Their intersection is $J$, so $\rad A=\Bbbk e_{12}\neq0$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Graded algebras}

\begin{definition}\label{def:group-grading}
Let $G$ be a group with identity $e$.
A \defn{$G$-graded $\Bbbk$-algebra} is a $\Bbbk$-algebra $A$ with $1_A\in A_e$ and a decomposition
\[
A=\bigoplus_{g\in G}A_g
\quad\text{such that}\quad
A_gA_h\subset A_{gh}
\quad\text{for all }g,h\in G.
\]
An element of $A_g$ is \defn{homogeneous of degree $g$}.
\end{definition}

\begin{convention}
Unless stated otherwise, a graded algebra is $\Z$-graded and \uline{concentrated in non-negative degrees}.
\end{convention}

Note that the decomposition is into a direct sum, so every element of a graded algebra $A$ can always be written as a \emph{finite} sum of homogeneous elements.

\begin{example}\label{ex:triangular-grading}
For the upper triangular matrix ring $\Bbbk A_n$ of Example~\ref{ex:triangular-algebra}, the decomposition
\[
(\Bbbk A_n)_d=\operatorname{span}_\Bbbk\{e_{ij}\mid j-i=d\}
\]
makes $\Bbbk A_n$ a graded algebra.
\end{example}


\begin{example}
The algebra $\Bbbk A_3$ also has a $\Z^{\oplus 2}$-grading with
\[
\deg e_{ii}=(0,0),\qquad
\deg e_{12}=(1,0),\qquad
\deg e_{23}=(0,1),\qquad
\deg e_{13}=(1,1).
\]
The homomorphism $\Z^{\oplus 2}\to\Z$, $(p,q)\mapsto p+q$, gives the grading of Example~\ref{ex:triangular-grading}.
Alternatively, setting $\deg e_{ij}=2(j-i)$ gives a $\Z$-grading with $A_1=0$ but $A_2\neq 0$.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}
A \defn{homomorphism of graded algebras} $f:A\to B$ is a $\Bbbk$-algebra homomorphism with $f(A_i)\subset B_i$ for all $i\in\Z$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
A graded algebra $A$ is \defn{locally finite} if $\dim_\Bbbk A_i<\infty$ for every $i\in\Z$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Graded modules}

\begin{definition}
Let $A=\bigoplus_{g\in G}A_g$ be a $G$-graded algebra.
A \defn{$G$-graded right $A$-module} is a right $A$-module $M$ with a direct sum decomposition
\[
M=\bigoplus_{g\in G}M_g
\quad\text{such that}\quad
M_gA_h\subset M_{gh}
\quad\text{for all }g,h\in G.
\]
An element of $M_g$ is called \defn{homogeneous of degree $g$}.

A $G$-graded $A$-module $M=\bigoplus_{g\in G} M_g$ is \defn{locally finite} if $\dim_{\Bbbk} M_g <\infty$ for all $g\in G$.
\end{definition}

Unless specified otherwise, graded modules are $\Z$-graded; their degrees need not be non-negative.

The \defn{opposite algebra} $A^\op$ has the same vector space as $A$, with multiplication $a^\op b^\op=(ba)^\op$.
A left $A$-module is a right $A^\op$-module by $m\cdot a^\op=am$.
For a $\Z$-graded algebra, $(A^\op)_i=A_i$.
Thus $\Mod(A^\op)$ is the category of left $A$-modules and left $A$-linear maps.
It is not the opposite category $(\Mod A)^\op$: the latter keeps right modules as objects and reverses the morphisms.

\begin{definition}\label{def:bimodule}
An \defn{$A$-bimodule} is a vector space $M$ with $\Bbbk$-bilinear left and right $A$-module actions satisfying $(am)b=a(mb)$ for all $a,b\in A$ and $m\in M$.
It is graded if $M=\bigoplus_{j\in\Z}M_j$ and $A_iM_jA_k\subset M_{i+j+k}$ for all $i,j,k\in\Z$.
\end{definition}

The algebra $A$ is a graded $A$-bimodule by multiplication, and each $A_i$ is an $A_0$-bimodule.
Bilinearity forces the left and right scalar actions to agree: $(c1_A)m=cm=m(c1_A)$ for $c\in\Bbbk$.

\begin{example}
The decomposition $A=\bigoplus_{i\in\Z}A_i$ makes the right regular module $A_A$ a graded module.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}\label{def:shift}
Let $M$ be a graded $A$-module and let $d\in\Z$.
The \defn{shift} $M(d)$ is the graded $A$-module whose underlying $A$-module is $M$, but whose grading is
\[
M(d)_j=M_{j+d}.
\]
\end{definition}

By Definition~\ref{def:shift}, $1\in A_0=A(-d)_d$, so $A(-d)$ has a generator in degree $d$.
If $M$ is concentrated in degree $0$, then $M(d)$ is concentrated in degree $-d$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:shifted-row}
For $A=\Bbbk A_3$ with the grading of Example~\ref{ex:triangular-grading}, the module $P_1:=e_{11}A$ consists of matrices supported on the first row.
Its basis $e_{11},e_{12},e_{13}$ has degrees $0,1,2$, respectively; in $P_1(-2)$, the same basis has degrees $2,3,4$.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}
Let $M,N$ be graded $A$-modules.
A homomorphism $f:M\to N$ is \defn{homogeneous of degree $d$} if
$f(M_j)\subset N_{j+d}$ for all $j\in\Z$. A \defn{homomorphism of graded modules} means a homogeneous $A$-module homomorphism of degree $0$, also said to \defn{preserve degrees}.
\end{definition}

\begin{notation}\label{not:graded-hom}
For graded right $A$-modules $M,N$, $\Hom_A(M,N)$ denotes all right $A$-module homomorphisms, without a grading condition. The space of homomorphisms preserving degrees is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:graded-hom}
\Hom_A^{\Z}(M,N)=\{f\in\Hom_A(M,N)\mid f(M_j)\subset N_j\text{ for all }j\in\Z\}.
\end{equation}
Thus $\Hom_A^{\Z}(M,N(d))$ consists of homomorphisms $M\to N$ of degree $d$, since $N(d)_j=N_{j+d}$.
For left modules we use $\Hom_{A^\op}$ and $\Hom_{A^\op}^{\Z}$.
\end{notation}

We write $\Grmod A$ for the category of graded right $A$-modules with morphism spaces $\Hom_A^{\Z}(M,N)$, identity maps, and the usual composition.
Its full subcategory $\grmod A$ consists of locally finite modules; here $\grmod A$ does not mean finitely generated graded modules.
Forgetting the grading gives a functor $\Grmod A\to\Mod A$.
The distinction between its source and target is the grading on objects and the degree condition on morphisms.

For $d\in\Z$, the shift of Definition~\ref{def:shift} is a functor $(d):\Grmod A\to\Grmod A$.
It sends $f:M\to N$ to the same underlying map $f(d):M(d)\to N(d)$.
Since $M(d)(-d)=M$ and $f(d)(-d)=f$, the shift is an equivalence with inverse $(-d)$; it restricts to $\grmod A$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:homogeneous-hom-sum}
For graded right $A$-modules $M,N$, the following inclusion is an equality when $M$ is finitely generated:
\[
\bigoplus_{d\in\Z}\Hom_A^{\Z}(M,N(d))\subset\Hom_A(M,N).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $f\in\Hom_A(M,N)$, define $f_d(m)=f(m)_{j+d}$ when $m\in M_j$, and extend linearly.
For $a\in A_i$, we have $f_d(ma)=f(ma)_{j+i+d}=f(m)_{j+d}a$, so $f_d$ is $A$-linear of degree $d$.
The sum of the homogeneous Hom spaces is direct: on $M_j$, distinct degrees have values in distinct summands of $N$.
If $M$ is finitely generated, the homogeneous components of a finite generating set still generate $M$. Only finitely many $f_d$ are non-zero on those generators, hence on $M$, giving $f=\sum_d f_d$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:infinite-hom}
Take $S=\Bbbk e_1\oplus\Bbbk e_2$ in degree $0$, $M=\bigoplus_{i\geq0}(e_1S)(-i)$, and $N=e_1S$ in degree $0$.
The $S$-linear map $M\to N$ sending the copy of $e_1$ in every summand to $e_1$ has a non-zero component in every degree $-i$. It is not a finite sum of homogeneous maps.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:matrix-hom}
For $A=\Bbbk A_3$, left multiplication by $e_{12}$ gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
e_{22}A&\longrightarrow e_{11}A,\\[-2pt]
\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&u&v\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}
&\longmapsto
\begin{pmatrix}0&u&v\\0&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}
\qquad(u,v\in\Bbbk).
\end{aligned}
\]
The $u$ and $v$ entries have degrees $0,1$ in the source and $1,2$ in the target. Thus the map has degree $1$; after replacing the source by $e_{22}A(-1)$, it belongs to $\Hom_A^{\Z}(e_{22}A(-1),e_{11}A)$.
Right $A$-linearity follows from associativity of matrix multiplication.
\end{example}

\subsection{Homogeneous ideals and quotients}

\begin{definition}
Let $A=\bigoplus_{i\in\Z}A_i$ be a graded algebra.
A right ideal $I\subset A$ is \defn{homogeneous} if
\[
I=\bigoplus_{i\in\Z}(I\cap A_i).
\]
Equivalently, whenever $a\in I$ and $a=a_0+\cdots+a_N$ is its decomposition into homogeneous components, each $a_i$ also belongs to $I$.
\end{definition}

For quotient algebras we need two-sided ideals.
If $I$ is a homogeneous two-sided ideal, then
\[
A/I=\bigoplus_{i\in\Z} A_i/(I\cap A_i)
\]
is again a graded algebra.

\begin{example}\label{ex:quadratic-matrix-quotient}
In $A=\Bbbk A_3$ with the grading of Example~\ref{ex:triangular-grading}, the two-sided ideal $I=(e_{13})=\Bbbk e_{13}$ is homogeneous.
Writing $\overline e_{ij}$ for the image of $e_{ij}$,
\[
(A/I)_0\cong\Bbbk^{\oplus 3},\qquad
(A/I)_1=\Bbbk\overline e_{12}\oplus\Bbbk\overline e_{23},\qquad
(A/I)_d=0\quad(d\geq 2).
\]
In particular, $\overline e_{12}\overline e_{23}=0$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
In $A=\Bbbk A_2$, the subspace $I=\Bbbk(e_{22}+e_{12})$ is a right ideal: $Ie_{11}=Ie_{12}=0$ and $Ie_{22}=I$.
However, $e_{22},e_{12}\notin I$, so $I$ is not homogeneous.
In $A/I$, the non-zero element $\overline e_{12}=-\overline e_{22}$ lies in the images of both $A_1$ and $A_0$, so those images do not form a direct sum.
The right module $A/I$ therefore does not inherit the quotient grading.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}
The \defn{positive degree ideal} of a graded algebra $A$ is $A_{>0}:=\bigoplus_{i>0}A_i$.\footnote{It is also called the augmentation ideal; we avoid that terminology.}
\end{definition}

The subspace $A_{>0}$ is a homogeneous two-sided ideal. The quotient map
\[
\pi:A\longrightarrow A/A_{>0}\cong A_0,\qquad
\sum_{i\in\Z}a_i\mapsto a_0,
\]
makes $A_0$ a graded $A$-bimodule concentrated in degree $0$, with $a\cdot s\cdot b=\pi(a)s\pi(b)$ for $a,b\in A$ and $s\in A_0$.
Resolutions of this module $A_0$ will define Koszul algebras in Lecture~5.


\subsection{Tensor products of vector spaces}

\begin{definition}\label{def:vector-tensor}
For vector spaces $U,V$, the \defn{tensor product} $U\otimes_\Bbbk V$ is equipped with a bilinear map $(u,v)\mapsto u\otimes v$ such that every bilinear map $b:U\times V\to W$ has a unique linear factorisation:
\[
\xymatrix@C=60pt@R=22pt{
U\times V \ar[r]^{(u,v)\mapsto u\otimes v} \ar[dr]_{b}
& U\otimes_\Bbbk V \ar@{-->}[d]^{\exists!\,\widetilde b}\\
& W.
}
\]
\end{definition}

An element of the form $u\otimes v$ is called a \defn{pure tensor}.
To construct $U\otimes_\Bbbk V$, choose bases $(u_i)_{i\in I}$ and $(v_j)_{j\in J}$ and take the vector space with basis symbols $u_i\otimes v_j$.
Extend $(u_i,v_j)\mapsto u_i\otimes v_j$ bilinearly. A bilinear map $b$ determines exactly one linear map by $u_i\otimes v_j\mapsto b(u_i,v_j)$, proving the required property.
For two spaces satisfying Definition~\ref{def:vector-tensor}, the induced maps in both directions are inverse by uniqueness. Thus the construction is independent of the bases up to a unique isomorphism preserving pure tensors.
For finite bases of sizes $r,s$, we obtain $\dim_\Bbbk(U\otimes_\Bbbk V)=rs$.
We use the canonical identifications $(U\otimes_\Bbbk V)\otimes_\Bbbk W\cong U\otimes_\Bbbk(V\otimes_\Bbbk W)$ and $\Bbbk\otimes_\Bbbk U\cong U$, and write $U^{\otimes 0}=\Bbbk$.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Graded $\Bbbk$-linear duals}{Graded k-linear duals}}

A linear functional on a graded vector space need not have a degree: it may be non-zero on infinitely many homogeneous components.
To obtain a graded vector space, we retain only finite sums of homogeneous functionals.
The resulting dual reverses left and right graded module structures, as needed later for linear complexes and the BGG correspondence.

\begin{definition}\label{def:linear-duals}
For a vector space $V$, its \defn{$\Bbbk$-linear dual} is $D(V):=\Hom_\Bbbk(V,\Bbbk)$.
For a graded vector space $V=\bigoplus_{i\in\Z}V_i$, its \defn{graded $\Bbbk$-linear dual} is the graded vector space given by
\[
\bbD(V)=\bigoplus_{i\in \Z} \bbD(V)_i
\qquad\text{with}\qquad
\bbD(V)_i=D(V_{-i}).
\]
\end{definition}

For general infinite-dimensional $V$, we have $\bbD(V)\subset D(V)$, whereas $D(V)\cong\prod_{i\in\Z}D(V_i)$.
These spaces coincide when $V$ is finite-dimensional.

Linear duality reverses the side of a module action.
For a right $A$-module $M$ and a left $A$-module $N$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:linear-dual-actions}
(a f)(m):=f(ma),\qquad (g a)(n):=g(an)
\quad(a\in A,\ f\in D(M),\ g\in D(N)).
\end{equation}
Here $m\in M$ and $n\in N$; the first formula makes $D(M)$ a left module and the second makes $D(N)$ a right module.
For graded modules, the same formulas preserve the graded $\Bbbk$-linear dual: if $a\in A_p$ and $f\in\bbD(M)_q$, then $af$ vanishes outside $M_{-p-q}$ and hence has degree $p+q$.

For a module homomorphism $u:M\to N$, precomposition defines $D(u):D(N)\to D(M)$ by $D(u)(f)=f\circ u$.
If $u$ preserves degrees, this restricts to $\bbD(u):\bbD(N)\to\bbD(M)$, also of degree $0$.
Thus we obtain functors
\begin{equation}\label{eq:linear-dual-functors}
D:(\Mod A)^\op\longrightarrow\Mod(A^\op),
\qquad
\bbD:(\Grmod A)^\op\longrightarrow\Grmod(A^\op).
\end{equation}
Indeed, $D(\id_M)=\id_{D(M)}$ and $D(v\circ u)=D(u)\circ D(v)$ for composable $u,v$, and the same identities hold for $\bbD$.
Replacing $A$ by $A^\op$ gives the corresponding functors from left modules to right modules.

\begin{example}\label{ex:row-linear-dual}
Let $A=\Bbbk A_3$ and $P_1=e_{11}A$ as in Example~\ref{ex:shifted-row}.
The functionals $\varepsilon_{1j}(e_{1\ell})=\delta_{j\ell}$ for $1\leq j,\ell\leq 3$ form a basis of $\bbD(P_1)$, with
\[
\deg\varepsilon_{1j}=1-j,\qquad
e_{12}\varepsilon_{12}=\varepsilon_{11},\qquad
e_{23}\varepsilon_{13}=\varepsilon_{12},\qquad
e_{jj}\varepsilon_{1j}=\varepsilon_{1j}.
\]
The actions follow from \eqref{eq:linear-dual-actions}.
For comparison with an infinite-dimensional space, take $V=\bigoplus_{i\geq 0}\Bbbk v_i$ with $\deg v_i=i$.
The functional $\lambda(v_i)=1$ for every $i$ belongs to $D(V)$ but not to $\bbD(V)$.
\end{example}

Recall that $0\to L\xrightarrow{i}M\xrightarrow{p}N\to0$ is \defn{short exact} if $i$ is injective, $p$ is surjective, and $\Im i=\Ker p$.
In $\Grmod A$ the maps must preserve degrees; exactness can then be checked in each degree.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:linear-bidual}
The functor $\bbD$ in \eqref{eq:linear-dual-functors} restricts to a duality $(\grmod A)^\op\to\grmod(A^\op)$.
Evaluation gives a natural isomorphism
\begin{equation}\label{eq:linear-bidual-evaluation}
\mathrm{ev}_M:M\longrightarrow\bbD(\bbD(M)),
\qquad m\longmapsto(f\mapsto f(m))
\quad(M\in\grmod A).
\end{equation}
Moreover, $\bbD$ takes every short exact sequence $0\to L\to M\to N\to0$ to a short exact sequence $0\to\bbD(N)\to\bbD(M)\to\bbD(L)\to0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The evaluation map \eqref{eq:linear-bidual-evaluation} is an isomorphism in each degree by finite-dimensional linear algebra, and \eqref{eq:linear-dual-actions} makes it $A$-linear.
For $u:M\to N$ of degree $0$, $m\in M$, and $f\in\bbD(N)$,
\[
\bigl(\bbD(\bbD(u))(\mathrm{ev}_M(m))\bigr)(f)
=\mathrm{ev}_M(m)(f\circ u)=f(u(m))
=\mathrm{ev}_N(u(m))(f).
\]
Hence $\bbD(\bbD(u))\circ\mathrm{ev}_M=\mathrm{ev}_N\circ u$, which is naturality as in \eqref{eq:naturality-square}.
Evaluation on left modules gives the other natural isomorphism required for a duality.
Finally, dualising a short exact sequence in each degree is exact, since every linear functional on a subspace extends to the whole vector space.
\end{proof}

The same argument without grading gives a duality $D:(\mod A)^\op\to\mod(A^\op)$.
Without finite dimensionality for $D$, or local finiteness for $\bbD$, evaluation need not be surjective. These duality statements require the finiteness hypotheses.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Left, right, and two-sided duals over $S$}{Left, right, and two-sided duals over S}}

Fix a finite-dimensional semisimple $\Bbbk$-algebra $S$, concentrated in degree $0$.
Besides the $\Bbbk$-linear dual, we need duals defined by left $S$-linear maps, right $S$-linear maps, and maps respecting both actions.
Their definitions differ, even though the resulting spaces can be identified after a choice of form.
The $S$-duals retain $S$-valued evaluation without choosing such a form. Their tensor and biduality isomorphisms in Proposition~\ref{prop:tensor-duals} and Lemma~\ref{lem:S-bidual} therefore construct quadratic duality without a choice of form; replacing them throughout by $D$ requires an identification $S\cong D(S)$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:S-duals}
For a right $S$-module $M$ and a left $S$-module $N$, define their \defn{$S$-duals} by
\[
\begin{aligned}
M^*&:=\Hom_S(M,S),&
(sf)(m)&:=sf(m),\\
{}^*N&:=\Hom_{S^\op}(N,S),&
(gs)(n)&:=g(n)s.
\end{aligned}
\]
Here $s\in S$, $m\in M$, $n\in N$, $f\in M^*$, and $g\in{}^*N$.
\end{definition}

Thus $M^*$ is a left $S$-module and $ {}^*N$ is a right $S$-module.
The position of the star records the side of the original action: $f(ms)=f(m)s$ in $M^*$, whereas $g(sn)=sg(n)$ in $ {}^*N$.
For a right module map $u:M\to N$, define $u^*:N^*\to M^*$ by $u^*(f)=f\circ u$; for a left module map $u:M\to N$, the same recipe defines ${}^*u:{}^*N\to{}^*M$.
Precomposition gives functors
\[
(-)^*:(\Mod S)^\op\longrightarrow\Mod(S^\op),
\qquad
{}^*(-):(\Mod(S^\op))^\op\longrightarrow\Mod S.
\]

\begin{definition}\label{def:enveloping-algebra}
The \defn{enveloping algebra} of $S$ is $S^{\mathrm{en}}:=S\otimes_\Bbbk S^\op$, with multiplication
\[
(s\otimes t^\op)(s'\otimes (t')^\op)=ss'\otimes(t't)^\op
\quad(s,t,s',t'\in S).
\]
\end{definition}

An $S$-bimodule is equivalently a right $S^{\mathrm{en}}$-module via
\begin{equation}\label{eq:enveloping-action}
m\cdot(s\otimes t^\op)=tms
\quad(s,t\in S,\ m\in M).
\end{equation}
The first tensor factor acts on the right because we are using right $S^{\mathrm{en}}$-modules; the second records the left $S$-action.
For example, $(m\cdot(s\otimes t^\op))\cdot(s'\otimes(t')^\op)=t'tmss'=m\cdot(ss'\otimes(t't)^\op)$.
A morphism of $S$-bimodules is a linear map preserving both actions.
Such a map is exactly a right $S^{\mathrm{en}}$-module homomorphism under \eqref{eq:enveloping-action}, so this identifies the category of $S$-bimodules with $\Mod S^{\mathrm{en}}$.
All three spaces $D(M)$, $M^*$, and $ {}^*M$ inherit bimodule structures:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bimodule-dual-actions}
(s\lambda t)(m)=\lambda(tms),\qquad
(sft)(m)=sf(tm),\qquad
(sgt)(m)=g(ms)t,
\end{equation}
where $\lambda\in D(M)$, $f\in M^*$, $g\in{}^*M$, $s,t\in S$, and $m\in M$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:en-dual}
The \defn{two-sided dual} of an $S$-bimodule $M$ is
\[
M^{*_{\mathrm{en}}}:=\Hom_{S^{\mathrm{en}}}(M,S^{\mathrm{en}}),
\qquad (sFt)(m):=(s\otimes t^\op)F(m)
\quad(s,t\in S,\ F\in M^{*_{\mathrm{en}}},\ m\in M).
\]
\end{definition}

In Definition~\ref{def:en-dual}, $M$ has the right action \eqref{eq:enveloping-action}, so $F(tms)=F(m)(s\otimes t^\op)$.
The output is a left $S^{\mathrm{en}}$-module by multiplication on the values, which gives the displayed bimodule action.
The two-sided dual takes values in $S^{\mathrm{en}}$, not in $S$.
On bimodule morphisms it acts by precomposition, as do $D$, $(-)^*$, and ${}^*(-)$; each is a contravariant functor on the category of $S$-bimodules.
It is useful when dualising maps with two module actions at once. The two-sided comparisons are included for reference; the construction of quadratic duals uses only the left and right $S$-duals. See \cite[\S2.1]{GI20} for applications to bimodule resolutions.

\subsection{Comparing the duals}

An $S$-dual consists of $S$-valued maps; the $\Bbbk$-linear dual consists of scalar-valued maps. A suitable linear functional $S\to\Bbbk$ identifies these constructions.

\begin{definition}\label{def:symmetrising-form}
A \defn{symmetrising form} on a finite-dimensional $\Bbbk$-algebra $S$ is a linear map $\tau:S\to\Bbbk$ satisfying
\[
\tau(st)=\tau(ts)\quad\text{for all }s,t\in S,
\qquad
\bigl(\tau(st)=0\text{ for all }t\in S\bigr)\ \Longrightarrow\ s=0.
\]
An algebra admitting such a form is \defn{symmetric}.
\end{definition}

Every finite-dimensional semisimple $\Bbbk$-algebra is symmetric, over an arbitrary field; see \cite[Introduction]{DNN17} for this theorem and its original reference.
We use the theorem without proof and do not restrict $S$ to products of matrix algebras over $\Bbbk$.
The forms needed in our basic examples are explicit.

\begin{example}\label{ex:symmetrising-form}
For $S=\Bbbk^{\oplus r}$, take $\tau(a_1,\ldots,a_r)=\sum_i a_i$.
For $S=\Mat_n(\Bbbk)$, take $\tau(a)=\Tr(a)$.
Indeed, $\Tr(ab)=\Tr(ba)$ and $\Tr(ae_{ji})=a_{ij}$, so the pairing is non-degenerate in every characteristic.
More generally, the sum of the matrix traces works on every product $\prod_{j=1}^r\Mat_{n_j}(\Bbbk)$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:artin-wedderburn}.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Dual comparison, {\cite[\S2.1]{GI20}}]\label{prop:dual-comparison}
Choose a symmetrising form $\tau$ on $S$.
For every finite-dimensional $S$-bimodule $M$, there are isomorphisms of $S$-bimodules
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dual-comparison}
\alpha_M:M^*\longrightarrow D(M),\quad f\longmapsto\tau\circ f,
\qquad
\beta_M:{}^*M\longrightarrow D(M),\quad g\longmapsto\tau\circ g.
\end{equation}
There is also an isomorphism of $S$-bimodules $\gamma_M:M^{*_{\mathrm{en}}}\to M^*$ given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:en-dual-comparison}
\gamma_M(F)(m)=\sum_j a_j\tau(b_j)
\quad\text{where}\quad
F(m)=\sum_j a_j\otimes b_j^\op.
\end{equation}
For fixed $\tau$, these are natural isomorphisms of contravariant functors on finite-dimensional $S$-bimodules.
For example, every bimodule map $u:M\to N$ gives a commuting square
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dual-comparison-naturality}
\xymatrix@C=48pt@R=20pt{
N^* \ar[r]^{\alpha_N} \ar[d]_{u^*} & D(N) \ar[d]^{D(u)}\\
M^* \ar[r]_{\alpha_M} & D(M).
}
\end{equation}
The corresponding squares for $\beta$ and $\gamma$ also commute.
The maps $\alpha_M$ and $\beta_M$ also apply to right and left $S$-modules, respectively, with the opposite side on the duals.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Non-degeneracy makes $S\to D(S)$, $a\mapsto(s\mapsto\tau(as))$, injective, hence bijective because both spaces have the same finite dimension.
Thus for $\lambda\in D(M)$ there exist unique $f(m),g(m)\in S$ satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dual-inverses}
\tau(f(m)s)=\lambda(ms),\qquad
\tau(sg(m))=\lambda(sm)
\quad(s\in S,\ m\in M).
\end{equation}
The inverse of $S\to D(S)$ is linear, so $m\mapsto f(m)$ and $m\mapsto g(m)$ are linear.
Equation~\eqref{eq:dual-inverses} gives $f(ms)=f(m)s$, $g(sm)=sg(m)$, and $\alpha_M(f)=\beta_M(g)=\lambda$.
Hence $\alpha_M,\beta_M$ are bijective.
For bimodule compatibility, use \eqref{eq:bimodule-dual-actions} and
\[
\tau(sf(tm))=\tau(f(tm)s)=\tau(f(tms)),\qquad
\tau(g(ms)t)=\tau(tg(ms))=\tau(g(tms)).
\]

The form $\tau_{\mathrm{en}}(a\otimes b^\op)=\tau(a)\tau(b)$ is symmetrising on $S^{\mathrm{en}}$.
Indeed, $\tau_{\mathrm{en}}((a\otimes b^\op)(c\otimes d^\op))=\tau(ac)\tau(db)=\tau(ca)\tau(bd)$, and its pairing is the tensor product of two non-degenerate pairings.
The formula \eqref{eq:en-dual-comparison} satisfies
\[
\gamma_M(F)(ms)=\gamma_M(F)(m)s,\qquad
\gamma_M(sFt)=s\gamma_M(F)t,\qquad
\alpha_M(\gamma_M(F))=\tau_{\mathrm{en}}\circ F.
\]
The argument \eqref{eq:dual-inverses}, applied to $S^{\mathrm{en}}$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{en}}$, makes $F\mapsto\tau_{\mathrm{en}}\circ F$ an isomorphism.
Thus $\gamma_M$ is an isomorphism.
For \eqref{eq:dual-comparison-naturality}, both routes send $f\in N^*$ to $\tau\circ f\circ u$; the checks for $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are identical.
\end{proof}

The identifications \eqref{eq:dual-comparison} and \eqref{eq:en-dual-comparison} depend on $\tau$.
For instance, $\tau(a_1,\ldots,a_r)=\sum_i c_i a_i$ is symmetrising on $\Bbbk^{\oplus r}$ for any $c_i\neq 0$.
No semisimplicity assumption on $S^{\mathrm{en}}$ was used.

\begin{definition}\label{def:graded-S-duals}
For a graded $S$-bimodule $M$, define its \defn{graded $S$-duals} and \defn{graded two-sided dual} by
\[
M^{(*)}:=\bigoplus_{i\in\Z}(M_{-i})^*,\qquad
{}^{(*)}M:=\bigoplus_{i\in\Z}{}^*(M_{-i}),\qquad
M^{(*_{\mathrm{en}})}:=\bigoplus_{i\in\Z}(M_{-i})^{*_{\mathrm{en}}},
\]
where each indicated summand has degree $i$.
The first construction also applies to graded right $S$-modules, and the second to graded left $S$-modules.
\end{definition}

On degree $0$ maps, the graded duals act by precomposition in each degree, so they are contravariant functors on the corresponding categories of graded modules.

Parentheses on the stars distinguish the graded constructions from the full $S$-duals. In the notation \eqref{eq:graded-hom},
\[
M^{(*)}=\bigoplus_{i\in\Z}\Hom_S^{\Z}(M,S(i)),\qquad
{}^{(*)}M=\bigoplus_{i\in\Z}\Hom_{S^\op}^{\Z}(M,S(i)).
\]

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:graded-dual-comparison}
For a fixed symmetrising form, the graded dual functors on the category of locally finite graded $S$-bimodules are naturally isomorphic:
\[
M^{(*)}\cong\bbD(M)\cong{}^{(*)}M\cong M^{(*_{\mathrm{en}})}.
\]
In particular, $\bbD(M)\cong M^{(*)}$ for a graded right $S$-module, and $\bbD(M)\cong{}^{(*)}M$ for a graded left $S$-module.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Apply Proposition~\ref{prop:dual-comparison} to $M_{-i}$ and take the direct sum over $i\in\Z$.
Every comparison preserves degree because $\tau$ has degree $0$.
Naturality is the degreewise version of \eqref{eq:dual-comparison-naturality}.
\end{proof}

For a graded algebra $A$ with $S=A_0$, Corollary~\ref{cor:graded-dual-comparison} transports the $A$-action \eqref{eq:linear-dual-actions} to the graded $S$-duals of a locally finite graded $A$-module:
\[
af=\alpha_M^{-1}(a\alpha_M(f))\quad\text{for right }M,
\qquad
ga=\beta_M^{-1}(\beta_M(g)a)\quad\text{for left }M.
\]
Here $\alpha_M(f)=\tau\circ f$ and $\beta_M(g)=\tau\circ g$ are the restrictions of \eqref{eq:dual-comparison}; $a\in A$, $f\in M^{(*)}$, and $g\in{}^{(*)}M$.
The restrictions to $S$ agree with Definition~\ref{def:S-duals}.
These remain duals over $S$, not duals taking values in $A$.
The graded $S$-bimodules are defined without $\tau$, but these transported $A$-actions depend on $\tau$. The canonical graded $A$-module is $\bbD(M)$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:transported-dual-action}
Let $A=\Bbbk A_2$, $S=\Bbbk e_{11}\oplus\Bbbk e_{22}$, and $M=e_{11}A$.
Take $\tau(e_{11})=c_1$ and $\tau(e_{22})=c_2$ with $c_1,c_2\neq0$.
The right $S$-linear maps $\varphi_0,\varphi_1\in M^{(*)}$ are specified by $\varphi_0(e_{11})=e_{11}$ and $\varphi_1(e_{12})=e_{22}$, with their other basis values zero. Their degrees are $0,-1$, and
\[
\alpha_M(e_{12}\varphi_1)(e_{11})
=\alpha_M(\varphi_1)(e_{12})=c_2,
\qquad e_{12}\varphi_1=\frac{c_2}{c_1}\varphi_0.
\]
Thus changing the form can change the action on the same vector space. Two choices give isomorphic $A$-modules by composing their identifications with $\bbD(M)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:dual-blocks}
Let $S=\Bbbk^{\oplus r}$ with coordinate idempotents $e_1,\ldots,e_r$ and the form $\tau(e_i)=1$.
When $S$ is realised as diagonal matrices, $e_i$ means the matrix unit $e_{ii}$.
For an $S$-bimodule $M$, the decompositions and the reversal of sides are
\[
M=\bigoplus_{i,j}e_iMe_j,
\qquad
e_jD(M)e_i\cong D(e_iMe_j)\cong e_jM^*e_i\cong e_j({}^*M)e_i.
\]
For the degree $1$ piece $V=\Bbbk e_{12}$ of $\Bbbk A_2$, we have $e_1Ve_2=V$.
Its graded $\Bbbk$-linear dual is spanned by $\varepsilon$ of degree $-1$, with $\varepsilon(e_{12})=1$ and $e_2\varepsilon e_1=\varepsilon$.
The corresponding elements of $V^*$ and $ {}^*V$ send $e_{12}$ to $e_2$ and $e_1$, respectively.
Thus equal scalar-valued functionals can correspond to different $S$-valued maps.
\end{example}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:S-bidual}
For finite-dimensional right $S$-modules $M$ and left $S$-modules $N$, evaluation gives natural isomorphisms $M\cong{}^*(M^*)$ and $N\cong({}^*N)^*$, sending $m$ to $(f\mapsto f(m))$ and $n$ to $(g\mapsto g(n))$, respectively.
For bimodules, both are bimodule isomorphisms.
Consequently, $(-)^*$ and ${}^*(-)$ give inverse dualities between $\mod S$ and $\mod(S^\op)$, without choosing a symmetrising form.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By Corollary~\ref{cor:semisimple-summands}, every finite-dimensional $S$-module is a direct summand of some $S^{\oplus n}$.
Evaluation is an isomorphism for $S$, hence for such summands; \eqref{eq:bimodule-dual-actions} gives bimodule compatibility.
For $u:M\to N$ and $f\in N^*$, evaluation at $m\in M$ gives $(u^*f)(m)=f(u(m))$, proving naturality. The left module calculation is the same.
\end{proof}

\section{Lecture 2: Tensor and quadratic algebras}

\subsection{Tensor products over an algebra}

Let $A$ be a graded algebra and set $S=A_0$. The degree zero algebra $S$ acts on both sides of $A_1$.
We therefore need the tensor product over an algebra, not only the tensor product over $\Bbbk$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:module-tensor}
Let $M$ be a right $S$-module and let $N$ be a left $S$-module.
The \defn{tensor product} $M\otimes_S N$ is the quotient of $M\otimes_\Bbbk N$ by the subspace generated by
\[
ms\otimes n-m\otimes sn
\qquad (m\in M,\ n\in N,\ s\in S).
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Basic properties of tensor products]\label{lem:tensor-properties}
\begin{enumerate}
\item For a vector space $W$, a bilinear map $b:M\times N\to W$ factors uniquely through $M\otimes_S N$ if and only if $b(ms,n)=b(m,sn)$ for all $m\in M$, $s\in S$, and $n\in N$.
\item For $S$-bimodules $M,N$, the tensor product is an $S$-bimodule with $s(m\otimes n)t=sm\otimes nt$.
\item For $S$-bimodules $U,V,W$, there are bimodule isomorphisms
\[
\begin{aligned}
(U\otimes_S V)\otimes_S W&\xrightarrow{\sim}U\otimes_S(V\otimes_S W),
&(u\otimes v)\otimes w&\longmapsto u\otimes(v\otimes w),\\
S\otimes_S V&\xrightarrow{\sim}V,
&s\otimes v&\longmapsto sv,\\
V\otimes_S S&\xrightarrow{\sim}V,
&v\otimes s&\longmapsto vs.
\end{aligned}
\]
\item Tensor products distribute over direct sums in either variable; for example,
$ (\bigoplus_i M_i)\otimes_S N\cong\bigoplus_i(M_i\otimes_S N)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
In (1), Definition~\ref{def:vector-tensor} gives a unique map $M\otimes_\Bbbk N\to W$; it descends to the quotient exactly when it kills the relations in Definition~\ref{def:module-tensor}.
The action in (2) preserves those relations because the two actions on each bimodule commute.
For (3), apply (1) to the displayed maps and their inverses; the unit inverses are $v\mapsto1\otimes v$ and $v\mapsto v\otimes1$.
For (4), apply (1) to $((m_i)_i,n)\mapsto(m_i\otimes n)_i$; the inverse is induced by the inclusions $M_i\to\bigoplus_i M_i$. Both composites fix pure tensors. The argument for the other variable is identical.
\end{proof}

The equality $ms\otimes_S n=m\otimes_S sn$ allows a scalar from $S$ to pass between adjacent factors.
For an $S$-bimodule $V$, define
\[
V^{\otimes_S 0}:=S,\qquad
V^{\otimes_S i}:=
\underbrace{V\otimes_S\cdots\otimes_S V}_{i\text{ factors}}
\quad (i\geq 1).
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:tensor-properties} makes these $S$-bimodules independent of parenthesisation and supplies the identifications used in concatenation.

Tensor duality reverses the order of the factors.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:tensor-duals}
Let $S$ be finite-dimensional semisimple and let $V,W$ be finite-dimensional $S$-bimodules.
There are bimodule isomorphisms compatible with bimodule maps in $V,W$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tensor-duals}
\begin{aligned}
\theta_D &: D(W)\otimes_S V^*\xrightarrow{\sim}D(V\otimes_S W),\\
\theta_r &: W^*\otimes_S V^*\xrightarrow{\sim}(V\otimes_S W)^*,\\
\theta_\ell &: {}^*W\otimes_S {}^*V\xrightarrow{\sim}{}^*(V\otimes_S W),\\
\theta_{\mathrm{en}} &: W^*\otimes_S V^{*_{\mathrm{en}}}
\xrightarrow{\sim}(V\otimes_S W)^{*_{\mathrm{en}}},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
defined by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tensor-evaluations}
\begin{aligned}
\theta_D(\lambda\otimes f)(v\otimes w)&=\lambda(f(v)w),\\
\theta_r(g\otimes f)(v\otimes w)&=g(f(v)w),\\
\theta_\ell(g\otimes f)(v\otimes w)&=f(vg(w)),\\
\theta_{\mathrm{en}}(g\otimes F)(v\otimes w)&=\sum_j g(a_jw)\otimes b_j^\op,
\quad\text{where }F(v)=\sum_j a_j\otimes b_j^\op.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The formulas in \eqref{eq:tensor-evaluations} respect the tensor relations and bimodule actions.
The maps $\theta_D$ and $\theta_r$ are isomorphisms for $V=S$ as a right module, hence for finite direct sums and direct summands of $S$.
Corollary~\ref{cor:semisimple-summands} applies to $V$.
For $\theta_\ell$, apply the same argument to the left module $W$.
Choose a symmetrising form $\tau$ as in Definition~\ref{def:symmetrising-form}, and use $\gamma_V,\gamma_{V\otimes_S W}$ from \eqref{eq:en-dual-comparison}.
Then
\[
\gamma_{V\otimes_S W}\circ\theta_{\mathrm{en}}
=\theta_r\circ(\id_{W^*}\otimes\gamma_V),
\]
since both sides evaluate $g\otimes F$ at $v\otimes w$ as $\sum_j g(a_jw)\tau(b_j)$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:dual-comparison} makes both $\gamma$ maps isomorphisms, so $\theta_{\mathrm{en}}$ is an isomorphism.
\end{proof}

The maps $\theta_r,\theta_\ell$ are the evaluations needed for quadratic duality \cite[\S2.7]{BGS96}. The maps $\theta_D,\theta_{\mathrm{en}}$ record their compatibility with linear and two-sided duals \cite[\S2.1]{GI20}; their extra formulas may be kept as reference material.

For a fixed symmetrising form $\tau$, let $\alpha_M(f)=\tau\circ f$ as in \eqref{eq:dual-comparison}.
Then tensor evaluation satisfies
\[
\alpha_{V\otimes_S W}\circ\theta_r
=\theta_D\circ(\alpha_W\otimes\id_{V^*}).
\]
Both sides send $g\otimes f$ to $v\otimes w\mapsto\tau(g(f(v)w))$.

\begin{example}
Let $S=\Bbbk^{\oplus r}$ with coordinate idempotents $e_1,\ldots,e_r$, and suppose $V=e_iVe_j$ and $W=e_jWe_\ell$.
By \eqref{eq:bimodule-dual-actions},
\[
V\otimes_S W=e_i(V\otimes_S W)e_\ell,\qquad
W^*\otimes_S V^*=e_\ell(W^*\otimes_S V^*)e_i.
\]
Thus $\theta_r$ in \eqref{eq:tensor-duals} reverses the two outer idempotents.
\end{example}

\subsection{Generated algebras}

Let $A$ be graded with $S=A_0$.
Multiplication makes $A_1$ an $S$-bimodule and gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:multiplication-maps}
\mu_i:A_1^{\otimes_S i}\longrightarrow A_i,\qquad
a_1\otimes\cdots\otimes a_i\longmapsto a_1\cdots a_i
\quad(i\geq 1),\qquad \mu_0=\id_S.
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}
The algebra $A$ is \defn{generated in degree $1$ over $S$} if every $\mu_i$ in \eqref{eq:multiplication-maps} is surjective.
\end{definition}

Equivalently, $S$ and $A_1$ generate $A$ as an algebra.

\begin{example}
The algebra $\Bbbk A_n$ of Example~\ref{ex:triangular-grading} is generated in degree $1$ over $S=\bigoplus_i\Bbbk e_{ii}$:
\[
e_{ij}=e_{i,i+1}e_{i+1,i+2}\cdots e_{j-1,j}
\quad(1\leq i<j\leq n).
\]
For the grading $\deg e_{ij}=2(j-i)$, the same algebra has $A_1=0$ and $A_2\neq 0$, so it is not generated in degree $1$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Tensor algebras and the universal property}

\begin{definition}\label{def:tensor-algebra}
Let $S$ be a $\Bbbk$-algebra and $V$ an $S$-bimodule.
The \defn{tensor algebra of $V$ over $S$} is
\[
T_S(V):=\bigoplus_{i\in\Z}T_S(V)_i,
\quad\text{where}\quad
T_S(V)_i=
\begin{cases}
V^{\otimes_S i},&i\geq 1,\\
S,&i=0,\\
0,&i<0,
\end{cases}
\]
with multiplication given by concatenation of tensors and the given $S$-actions.
\end{definition}

The left and right $S$-actions determine which factors can be concatenated.
Juxtaposition $v_1\cdots v_d$ in $T_S(V)$ denotes the tensor $v_1\otimes_S\cdots\otimes_S v_d$; in a specified quotient it denotes the image of that tensor.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:idempotent-tensors}
Let $S=\bigoplus_{i=1}^r\Bbbk e_i$ with coordinate idempotents and let $V,W$ be $S$-bimodules. There is a bimodule isomorphism
\begin{equation}\label{eq:idempotent-tensor-decomposition}
V\otimes_S W\cong\bigoplus_{i,j,\ell}e_iVe_j\otimes_\Bbbk e_jWe_\ell.
\end{equation}
Consequently, after choosing a basis of each $e_iVe_j$, a basis of $V^{\otimes_S d}$ for $d\geq1$ consists of the words of length $d$ whose adjacent idempotents match.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The mutually orthogonal idempotents sum to $1$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:composable-tensors}
V=\bigoplus_{i,j}e_iVe_j,\qquad
(e_iVe_j)\otimes_S(e_pWe_\ell)=0
\quad\text{if }j\neq p.
\end{equation}
For the second identity, $v\otimes w=ve_j\otimes w=v\otimes e_jw=0$.
The inclusions of the matching components define the map from right to left in \eqref{eq:idempotent-tensor-decomposition}. Its inverse is
\[
v\otimes_S w\longmapsto\sum_{i,j,\ell}e_ive_j\otimes_\Bbbk e_jwe_\ell.
\]
For $s=\sum_j s_je_j\in S$, replacing $(v,w)$ by $(vs,w)$ or $(v,sw)$ multiplies the $j$th summand by the same scalar $s_j$, so the inverse is well-defined.
Both composites are the identity by \eqref{eq:composable-tensors}. Iterating \eqref{eq:idempotent-tensor-decomposition} and using the vector-space tensor basis proves the assertion about words.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:tensor-A3}
Let $S=\Bbbk^{\oplus 3}$ with coordinate idempotents $e_1,e_2,e_3$, and let $V=\Bbbk a\oplus\Bbbk b$ have actions $e_1ae_2=a$ and $e_2be_3=b$, with all other idempotent actions zero.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:idempotent-tensors},
\[
V\otimes_S V=\Bbbk(a\otimes b),\qquad
V^{\otimes_S 3}=0,\qquad
T_S(V)=S\oplus\Bbbk a\oplus\Bbbk b\oplus\Bbbk ab.
\]
Here $ab=a\otimes b$.
The assignments $e_i\mapsto e_{ii}$, $a\mapsto e_{12}$, and $b\mapsto e_{23}$ give $T_S(V)\cong\Bbbk A_3$; the tensor $ab$ maps to $e_{13}$.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Universal property of the tensor algebra]\label{prop:tensor-universal}
Let $\iota_S:S\to T_S(V)$ and $\iota_V:V\to T_S(V)$ be the canonical inclusions.
Given a unital $\Bbbk$-algebra homomorphism $\varphi:S\to B$ and an $S$-bimodule map $f:V\to B$, where $sbt=\varphi(s)b\varphi(t)$ on $B$, there is a unique $\Bbbk$-algebra homomorphism $\widetilde f:T_S(V)\to B$ making the diagram commute:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tensor-universal}
\xymatrix@C=42pt@R=28pt{
V \ar[r]^{\iota_V} \ar[dr]_{f}
& T_S(V) \ar@{-->}[d]^{\exists!\,\widetilde f}
& S \ar[l]_{\iota_S} \ar[dl]^{\varphi}\\
& B. &
}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Define $\widetilde f$ linearly by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tensor-extension}
\widetilde f(s)=\varphi(s),\qquad
\widetilde f(v_1\otimes_S\cdots\otimes_S v_i)=f(v_1)\cdots f(v_i)
\quad(s\in S,\ i\geq 1).
\end{equation}
The identity $f(svt)=\varphi(s)f(v)\varphi(t)$ makes $\widetilde f$ well-defined on tensor products and compatible with multiplication.
Every homomorphism satisfying \eqref{eq:tensor-universal} has the values in \eqref{eq:tensor-extension}, proving uniqueness.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:tensor-presentation}
If $A$ is generated in degree $1$ over $S=A_0$, there is a surjective graded algebra homomorphism $\mu:T_S(A_1)\onto A$ whose degree $i$ component is $\mu_i$ from \eqref{eq:multiplication-maps}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Apply Proposition~\ref{prop:tensor-universal} to the inclusions $S\to A$ and $A_1\to A$.
Surjectivity of each $\mu_i$ gives surjectivity of $\mu$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:relations}
For the map $\mu$ in Corollary~\ref{cor:tensor-presentation}, the homogeneous two-sided ideal $I:=\Ker\mu$ is the \defn{ideal of relations}.
\end{definition}

Writing $V=A_1$, we obtain $A\cong T_S(V)/I$ with $I_0=I_1=0$.
The bimodule $V$ specifies the generators and their left and right $S$-actions; $I$ specifies the relations between their products.

\subsection{Quadratic algebras}

\begin{definition}[{\cite[Definition~1.2.2]{BGS96}}]\label{def:quadratic}
Let $S$ be finite-dimensional semisimple, $V$ a finite-dimensional $S$-bimodule, and $R\subset V\otimes_S V$ an $S$-sub-bimodule.
A graded algebra $A$ is \defn{quadratic} if it is isomorphic, as a graded algebra, to $T_S(V)/(R)$ for such $S,V,R$, where $(R)$ denotes the two-sided ideal generated by $R$ and $V$ has degree $1$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:quadratic-basic}
A quadratic algebra $A=T_S(V)/(R)$ has $A_0=S$, $A_1=V$, and $A_2=(V\otimes_S V)/R$, is locally finite, and is generated in degree $1$ over $S$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The ideal $(R)$ has no components in degrees $0$ and $1$.
Its degree $2$ component is exactly $R$.
Each $A_i$ is a quotient of the finite-dimensional space $V^{\otimes_S i}$, and multiplication maps $V^{\otimes_S i}$ onto $A_i$.
\end{proof}

Thus a quadratic presentation recovers $S\cong A_0$, $V\cong A_1$, and $R\cong\Ker(A_1\otimes_{A_0}A_1\to A_2)$.
In particular, the kernel of the canonical map $T_{A_0}(A_1)\onto A$ is generated by its degree $2$ component.
Quadraticity depends on the grading: for $n\geq2$, the grading $\deg e_{ij}=2(j-i)$ on $\Bbbk A_n$ is not quadratic, since it has no degree $1$ generators.

\begin{example}\label{ex:tensor-An}
Let $S=\bigoplus_{i=1}^n\Bbbk e_{ii}$ and $V=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n-1}\Bbbk e_{i,i+1}$ inside $\Bbbk A_n$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:idempotent-tensors}, the basis tensors of positive length are
\[
e_{i,i+1}\otimes_S e_{i+1,i+2}\otimes_S\cdots\otimes_S e_{j-1,j}
\longmapsto e_{ij}
\quad(1\leq i<j\leq n).
\]
Thus multiplication gives $T_S(V)\cong\Bbbk A_n$, a quadratic algebra with $R=0$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:zero-composition}
For $S,V,a,b$ from Example~\ref{ex:tensor-A3}, the quotient
\[
T_S(V)/(ab)\cong\Bbbk A_3/(e_{13})
\]
is quadratic with $R=\Bbbk(a\otimes b)$.
Its basis is $e_1,e_2,e_3,a,b$, in degrees $0,0,0,1,1$, as in Example~\ref{ex:quadratic-matrix-quotient}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:two-vertex-relations}
Let $S=\Bbbk^{\oplus 2}$ with coordinate idempotents $e_1,e_2$, and let $V=\Bbbk a\oplus\Bbbk b$ satisfy $e_1ae_2=a$ and $e_2be_1=b$, with all other idempotent actions zero.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:idempotent-tensors}, the alternating words in $a,b$ form the basis of positive degree tensors in $T_S(V)$.
The quadratic algebra $A=T_S(V)/(ab)$ has
\[
A=S\oplus\Bbbk a\oplus\Bbbk b\oplus\Bbbk ba,
\qquad \dim_\Bbbk A_0=2,\quad \dim_\Bbbk A_1=2,\quad \dim_\Bbbk A_2=1.
\]
Every alternating word of length at least $3$ contains $ab$, so all higher components vanish.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:cubic-quotient}
Let $B=\Bbbk A_4$ and $A=B/(e_{14})$, with the grading of Example~\ref{ex:triangular-grading}.
The ideal $I=(e_{14})=\Bbbk e_{14}$ has $I_2=0$ but $I_3\neq 0$.
Since $e_{14}=e_{12}e_{23}e_{34}$, the algebra $A$ is generated in degree $1$ with a relation of degree $3$ and no relations of degree $2$.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:quadratic-basic}, any quadratic presentation would have the same generators and zero quadratic relation space, hence would give $\Bbbk A_4$ itself. Thus $A$ is not quadratic.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Polynomial and exterior algebras]\label{ex:polynomial-exterior}
For the classical comparison, take $S=\Bbbk$ and $V=\bigoplus_{i=1}^n\Bbbk x_i$.
The \defn{symmetric algebra} and \defn{exterior algebra} are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:polynomial-exterior}
\begin{aligned}
\Symm(V)&=T_\Bbbk(V)/(x_ix_j-x_jx_i\mid i<j)
            \cong\Bbbk[x_1,\ldots,x_n],\\
\bigwedge V&=T_\Bbbk(V)/(v^2\mid v\in V).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Both are quadratic.
The polynomial basis allows repetitions; the exterior basis consists of $1$ and $x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_d}$ with $i_1<\cdots<i_d$.
The exterior relations are equivalently $x_i^2=0$ for $1\leq i\leq n$ and $x_ix_j+x_jx_i=0$ for $i<j$, in every characteristic.
\end{example}

The bases in Example~\ref{ex:polynomial-exterior} can be checked directly.
For $\Symm(V)$, commuting adjacent generators gives the ordered monomials, which are independent under the map to the polynomial ring.
For $\bigwedge V$, the relations reduce every word to a multiple of a strictly increasing word or to zero.
To prove independence in degree $d$, fix $i_1<\cdots<i_d$ and send $v_1\otimes\cdots\otimes v_d$ to the determinant of their coordinates in rows $i_1,\ldots,i_d$.
This functional vanishes on tensors with two equal adjacent factors, hence on the degree $d$ part of $(v^2\mid v\in V)$. On increasing basis words it is $1$ on $x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_d}$ and $0$ on the others, proving independence in every characteristic.

\subsection{Hilbert series}

A Hilbert series records the dimension of each homogeneous component.

\begin{definition}\label{def:hilbert-series}
Let $M=\bigoplus_{j\in\Z}M_j$ be a graded vector space with $\dim_\Bbbk M_j<\infty$ for all $j$ and $M_j=0$ for all $j\ll 0$.
Its \defn{Hilbert series} is the formal Laurent series $h_M(t):=\sum_{j\in\Z}(\dim_\Bbbk M_j)t^j$.
\end{definition}

For a locally finite graded algebra $A$, $h_A(t)$ refers to its underlying graded vector space; the series does not record multiplication.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:hilbert-operations}
Let $L,M,N$ be locally finite graded vector spaces concentrated in degrees bounded below.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A graded isomorphism $M\cong N$ implies $h_M(t)=h_N(t)$.
\item An exact sequence $0\to L\to M\to N\to0$ with degree $0$ maps gives $h_M(t)=h_L(t)+h_N(t)$.
\item For every $d\in\Z$, $h_{M(d)}(t)=t^{-d}h_M(t)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The first two statements follow from dimensions in each degree. For shifts,
\[
h_{M(d)}(t)=\sum_j\dim_\Bbbk(M_{j+d})t^j=t^{-d}\sum_i\dim_\Bbbk(M_i)t^i.\qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
For the upper triangular algebra in Example~\ref{ex:triangular-grading}, counting matrix entries on each diagonal gives
\[
h_{\Bbbk A_n}(t)=n+(n-1)t+\cdots+t^{n-1},\qquad
h_{\Bbbk A_3/(e_{13})}(t)=3+2t.
\]
The second equality also follows by subtracting $h_{(e_{13})}(t)=t^2$ from $h_{\Bbbk A_3}(t)$, as in Lemma~\ref{lem:hilbert-operations}(2).
For $A=T_S(V)/(ab)$ in Example~\ref{ex:two-vertex-relations}, the basis $e_1,e_2,a,b,ba$ gives $h_A(t)=2+2t+t^2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:hilbert-polynomial-exterior}
For the algebras in Example~\ref{ex:polynomial-exterior}, each polynomial generator may occur any number of times, whereas each exterior generator occurs at most once. Thus
\[
h_{\Symm(V)}(t)=\frac{1}{(1-t)^n},\qquad
h_{\bigwedge V}(t)=(1+t)^n
\quad\text{where }n=\dim_\Bbbk V.
\]
\end{example}

\begin{definition}\label{def:total-tensor-grading}
For graded vector spaces $V,W$, the \defn{total grading} on their tensor product is
\[
(V\otimes_\Bbbk W)_n=\bigoplus_{i+j=n}V_i\otimes_\Bbbk W_j.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:hilbert-tensor}
For locally finite graded vector spaces $V,W$ concentrated in degrees bounded below, $h_{V\otimes_\Bbbk W}(t)=h_V(t)h_W(t)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The coefficient of $t^n$ on either side is $\sum_{i+j=n}\dim_\Bbbk V_i\dim_\Bbbk W_j$. Only finitely many summands are non-zero because both gradings are bounded below.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The degree 2 relations of a quotient}

Let $S$ be finite-dimensional semisimple and $V$ a finite-dimensional $S$-bimodule.
Let $A=T_S(V)/I$ with $I_0=I_1=0$ as in Definition~\ref{def:relations}.
In degree $2$,
\[
A_2\cong(V\otimes_S V)/I_2,
\quad\text{where}\quad
I_2=I\cap(V\otimes_S V).
\]

\begin{definition}\label{def:quadratic-part}
The \defn{quadratic part} of $A$ is $qA:=T_S(V)/(I_2)$.
\end{definition}

The inclusion $(I_2)\subset I$ induces a surjective graded homomorphism $\pi_A:qA\onto A$, and
\[
\pi_A\text{ is an isomorphism}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad I=(I_2)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A\text{ is quadratic}.
\]

\begin{example}
For $A=\Bbbk A_4/(e_{14})$ from Example~\ref{ex:cubic-quotient}, we have $I_2=0$, so
\[
qA=\Bbbk A_4,\qquad
\pi_A:\Bbbk A_4\onto\Bbbk A_4/(e_{14}),\qquad
\Ker\pi_A=\Bbbk e_{14}.
\]
Thus $qA$ does not impose the cubic relation $e_{12}e_{23}e_{34}=0$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Orthogonal complements and quadratic duality}

\begin{definition}\label{def:orthogonals}
For an $S$-sub-bimodule $U$ of a finite-dimensional $S$-bimodule $M$, define the \defn{right orthogonal complement} and \defn{left orthogonal complement} by
\[
U^\perp:=\{f\in M^*\mid f(U)=0\},
\qquad
{}^\perp U:=\{g\in{}^*M\mid g(U)=0\}.
\]
\end{definition}

Both $U^\perp$ and $ {}^\perp U$ are $S$-sub-bimodules by \eqref{eq:bimodule-dual-actions}.
Fix a symmetrising form $\tau$ as in Definition~\ref{def:symmetrising-form}.
For the comparison maps $\alpha_M(f)=\tau\circ f$ and $\beta_M(g)=\tau\circ g$ in \eqref{eq:dual-comparison},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:orthogonal-comparison}
\alpha_M(U^\perp)=\beta_M({}^\perp U)
=\{\lambda\in D(M)\mid\lambda(U)=0\}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, if $\alpha_M(f)(U)=0$, then
\[
\tau(f(u)s)=\tau(f(us))=0
\quad\text{for all }s\in S,\ u\in U,
\]
so non-degeneracy of $\tau$ gives $f(U)=0$.
For $\beta_M$, use $\tau(sg(u))=\tau(g(su))$ instead.

\begin{example}\label{ex:symmetric-orthogonals}
For $\Symm(V)$ and $\bigwedge V$ in Example~\ref{ex:polynomial-exterior}, take $V=\Bbbk x\oplus\Bbbk y$.
The relation spaces are
\[
\begin{aligned}
R_{\Symm(V)}&=\Bbbk(x\otimes y-y\otimes x),\\
R_{\bigwedge V}&=\Bbbk(x\otimes x)\oplus\Bbbk(y\otimes y)\oplus\Bbbk(x\otimes y+y\otimes x).
\end{aligned}
\]
Let $x^*,y^*\in V^*$ be the coordinate functionals.
Identifying $(V\otimes_\Bbbk V)^*$ with $V^*\otimes_\Bbbk V^*$ using $\theta_r$ from \eqref{eq:tensor-evaluations},
\[
\begin{aligned}
R_{\Symm(V)}^\perp&=\operatorname{span}_\Bbbk\{x^*\otimes x^*,\,y^*\otimes y^*,\,x^*\otimes y^*+y^*\otimes x^*\},\\
R_{\bigwedge V}^\perp&=\Bbbk(x^*\otimes y^*-y^*\otimes x^*).
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus taking orthogonal complements exchanges the symmetric and exterior relation spaces.
\end{example}

\section{Lecture 3: The quadratic dual}\label{sec:quadratic-dual}

\subsection{Generators and orthogonal relations}

Throughout this lecture, $S$ is a finite-dimensional semisimple $\Bbbk$-algebra and $A=T_S(V)/(R)$ is quadratic, with $V$ finite-dimensional and $R\subset V\otimes_S V$ an $S$-sub-bimodule.
The quadratic dual records which linear functionals on products of two generators vanish on the relations. Proposition~\ref{prop:dual-low-degrees} makes the exchange explicit: its degree $2$ component is the $S$-dual of the relation space of $A$.
Recall that $V^*=\Hom_S(V,S)$ uses right $S$-linear maps, whereas ${}^*V=\Hom_{S^\op}(V,S)$ uses left $S$-linear maps.
The tensor evaluations in \eqref{eq:tensor-evaluations} are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quadratic-pairings}
\begin{aligned}
\theta_r(g\otimes f)(v\otimes w)&=g(f(v)w),
& f,g&\in V^*,\\
\theta_\ell(g\otimes f)(v\otimes w)&=f(vg(w)),
& f,g&\in{}^*V.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Both reverse the order of the factors. The quadratic dual replaces $V$ by an $S$-dual and $R$ by the maps vanishing on $R$.

\begin{convention}
We identify $V^*\otimes_S V^*$ with $(V\otimes_S V)^*$ via $\theta_r$, and ${}^*V\otimes_S{}^*V$ with ${}^*(V\otimes_S V)$ via $\theta_\ell$.
Thus $R^\perp$ and ${}^\perp R$ in tensor algebras mean the inverse images of the orthogonal complements in Definition~\ref{def:orthogonals} under these evaluations.
\end{convention}

\begin{definition}[{\cite[\S2.8]{BGS96}}]\label{def:quadratic-duals}
The \defn{right quadratic dual} and \defn{left quadratic dual} of $A$ are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quadratic-duals}
\begin{aligned}
A^!&:=T_S(V^*)/(R^\perp),
&R^\perp&=\{F\in V^*\otimes_S V^*\mid\theta_r(F)(R)=0\},\\
{}^!A&:=T_S({}^*V)/({}^\perp R),
&{}^\perp R&=\{F\in{}^*V\otimes_S{}^*V\mid\theta_\ell(F)(R)=0\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The generators $V^*$ and ${}^*V$ have degree $1$.
\end{definition}

We use $A^!$ unless a side is specified. The construction uses the ordinary $S$-dual of $V$, placed in degree $1$, not the graded $S$-dual $V^{(*)}$, which has degree $-1$.
It is independent of a chosen presentation: $V=A_1$ and $R=\Ker(A_1\otimes_S A_1\to A_2)$ are intrinsic to $A$.
An isomorphism \defn{over $S$} means one restricting to $\id_S$ on degree $0$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:dual-low-degrees}
The algebra $A^!$ is quadratic and
\[
A^!_0=S,\qquad A^!_1=V^*,\qquad A^!_2\cong R^*.
\]
In particular, $\dim_\Bbbk A^!_2=\dim_\Bbbk R$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Restriction to $R$ gives an exact sequence of $S$-bimodules
\begin{equation}\label{eq:restriction-relations}
0\longrightarrow R^\perp\longrightarrow V^*\otimes_S V^*
\xrightarrow{F\mapsto\theta_r(F)|_R}R^*\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
Surjectivity follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:artin-wedderburn}: $R$ has a complement as a right $S$-module, so a right $S$-linear map $R\to S$ extends by zero on that complement.
Now use Proposition~\ref{prop:quadratic-basic}; the dimension statement follows from Proposition~\ref{prop:dual-comparison}.
\end{proof}

Adding relations has the opposite effect after dualising. For $S$-sub-bimodules $R\subset R'\subset V\otimes_S V$,
\[
R\subset R'\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(R')^\perp\subset R^\perp
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\bigl(T_S(V)/(R')\bigr)^!\onto\bigl(T_S(V)/(R)\bigr)^!.
\]

\subsection{Recovering the original algebra}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:double-orthogonal}
Let $U\subset M$ be an $S$-sub-bimodule of a finite-dimensional $S$-bimodule. Under the evaluation isomorphisms of Lemma~\ref{lem:S-bidual},
\[
{}^\perp(U^\perp)=U,\qquad ({}^\perp U)^\perp=U.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Every $u\in U$ is annihilated by every $f\in U^\perp$, so $U\subset{}^\perp(U^\perp)$.
If $m\notin U$, then $m+U\neq0$ in $M/U$. By Corollary~\ref{cor:semisimple-summands}, $M/U$ embeds as a direct summand of some $S^{\oplus n}$ as a right module. At least one coordinate of the image of $m+U$ is non-zero, giving a right $S$-linear map $M/U\to S$ non-zero on $m+U$.
Its composite with $M\to M/U$ belongs to $U^\perp$ and does not vanish on $m$. Hence $m\notin{}^\perp(U^\perp)$.
The proof of $({}^\perp U)^\perp=U$ uses left modules instead.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:quadratic-bidual}
Left and right quadratic duality are canonically inverse:
\[
{}^!(A^!)\cong A\cong({}^!A)^!
\quad\text{as graded algebras over }S.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Generators.} Write $\operatorname{ev}_V:V\to{}^*(V^*)$, $\operatorname{ev}_V(v)(f)=f(v)$, for the isomorphism in Lemma~\ref{lem:S-bidual}. It identifies the generators of $A$ and ${}^!(A^!)$.

\emph{Relations.} We must check that the induced identification on tensors agrees with evaluation on $V\otimes_S V$. The square
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bidual-tensor-square}
\xymatrix@C=70pt@R=30pt{
V\otimes_S V \ar[r]^{\operatorname{ev}_V\otimes\operatorname{ev}_V} \ar[d]_{\operatorname{ev}_{V\otimes_S V}}
& {}^*(V^*)\otimes_S{}^*(V^*) \ar[d]^{\theta_\ell}\\
{}^*((V\otimes_S V)^*) \ar[r]_{F\mapsto F\circ\theta_r}
& {}^*(V^*\otimes_S V^*)
}
\end{equation}
commutes, where the right vertical map is \eqref{eq:quadratic-pairings} applied to the bimodule $V^*$.
Indeed, for $v,w\in V$ and $f,g\in V^*$, the right $S$-action on $V^*$ in \eqref{eq:bimodule-dual-actions} gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\theta_\ell(\operatorname{ev}_V(v)\otimes\operatorname{ev}_V(w))(g\otimes f)
&=\operatorname{ev}_V(w)(g f(v))\\
&=g(f(v)w)=\theta_r(g\otimes f)(v\otimes w).
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus, using the identifications \eqref{eq:quadratic-pairings}, the relations ${}^\perp(R^\perp)$ of ${}^!(A^!)$ correspond to the double annihilator of $R$ in $V\otimes_S V$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:double-orthogonal} identifies that annihilator with $R$, so \eqref{eq:quadratic-duals} gives ${}^!(A^!)\cong A$.
Interchanging left and right gives $({}^!A)^!\cong A$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:quadratic-dual-sides}
A choice of symmetrising form $\tau:S\to\Bbbk$ gives isomorphisms $A^!\cong{}^!A$ and $(A^!)^!\cong A$ of graded algebras over $S$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The maps $\alpha_V(f)=\tau\circ f$ and $\beta_V(g)=\tau\circ g$ in \eqref{eq:dual-comparison} give a bimodule isomorphism $\beta_V^{-1}\alpha_V:V^*\to{}^*V$.
For $f,g\in V^*$, write $f'=\beta_V^{-1}\alpha_V(f)$ and $g'=\beta_V^{-1}\alpha_V(g)$. Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
\tau\bigl(f'(vg'(w))\bigr)
&=\tau\bigl(f(v)g'(w)\bigr)
=\tau\bigl(g'(f(v)w)\bigr)\\
&=\tau\bigl(g(f(v)w)\bigr)
\qquad(v,w\in V).
\end{aligned}
\]
By \eqref{eq:orthogonal-comparison}, $(\beta_V^{-1}\alpha_V)^{\otimes_S2}$ therefore identifies $R^\perp$ with ${}^\perp R$.
It induces $A^!\cong{}^!A$ on the presentations \eqref{eq:quadratic-duals}.
Apply the same comparison to $A^!$ and then Theorem~\ref{thm:quadratic-bidual} to obtain $(A^!)^!\cong{}^!(A^!)\cong A$.
\end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{thm:quadratic-bidual} needs no choice of form. Proposition~\ref{prop:quadratic-dual-sides} permits the usual statement that quadratic duality is an involution, but the identification of left and right duals uses $\tau$.

\subsection{Tensor algebras and monomial relations}

\begin{example}\label{ex:tensor-square-zero-dual}
Taking $R=0$ or $R=V\otimes_S V$ in \eqref{eq:quadratic-duals} gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\bigl(T_S(V)\bigr)^!
&=T_S(V^*)/(V^*\otimes_S V^*)\cong S\oplus V^*,\\
\bigl(T_S(V)/(V\otimes_S V)\bigr)^!&=T_S(V^*).
\end{aligned}
\]
In $S\oplus V^*$, $(V^*)^2=0$ and $(s,f)(t,g)=(st,sg+ft)$.
Thus a tensor algebra with no relations is dual to an algebra in which every product of two positive degree elements is zero.
\end{example}

For the rest of this subsection, take $S=\bigoplus_{i=1}^r\Bbbk e_i$, with $e_ie_j=\delta_{ij}e_i$ and $\sum_i e_i=1$.
Choose a basis $\mathcal B_{ij}$ of each $e_iVe_j$ and let $\mathcal B=\bigcup_{i,j}\mathcal B_{ij}$.
A non-empty word $b_1\cdots b_d$ in $\mathcal B$ is \defn{composable} if its adjacent idempotents match. By Lemma~\ref{lem:idempotent-tensors}, these words form a basis of the positive degree part of $T_S(V)$.

\begin{definition}
The presentation $A=T_S(V)/(R)$ is \defn{monomial quadratic} with respect to $\mathcal B$ if $R$ is spanned by a subset $\mathcal R$ of the composable words of length $2$ in $\mathcal B$.
\end{definition}

For $b\in\mathcal B_{ij}$, define $b^*\in V^*$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dual-basis-idempotents}
b^*(c)=\delta_{bc}e_j\quad(c\in\mathcal B),
\qquad e_jb^*e_i=b^*.
\end{equation}
The elements $b^*$ form a basis of $V^*$; their left and right idempotents are reversed.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:monomial-dual}
For a monomial quadratic presentation:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A basis of $A$ consists of the $e_i$ and the non-empty composable words containing no adjacent pair from $\mathcal R$.
\item The quadratic dual is monomial, with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:monomial-dual}
R^\perp=\operatorname{span}_\Bbbk
\{c^*\otimes_S b^*\mid bc\text{ is composable and }bc\notin\mathcal R\}.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The ideal generated by $\mathcal R$ is spanned by the composable words containing an element of $\mathcal R$ as an adjacent pair. Removing those basis words proves (1).
For $b\in\mathcal B_{ij}$ and $c\in\mathcal B_{j\ell}$, \eqref{eq:quadratic-pairings} and \eqref{eq:dual-basis-idempotents} give
\[
\theta_r(c^*\otimes b^*)(b\otimes c)=c^*(e_jc)=e_\ell.
\]
The functional vanishes on every other basis tensor.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:idempotent-tensors}, the composable $c^*\otimes b^*$ form a basis of $V^*\otimes_S V^*$.
Evaluating a linear combination on each $b\otimes c\in\mathcal R$ forces precisely the coefficient of $c^*\otimes b^*$ to be zero, since $e_\ell\neq0$.
Thus the annihilator is the span of the complementary basis tensors, proving (2).
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:An-quadratic-dual}
For $A=\Bbbk A_n$, take $S=\bigoplus_i\Bbbk e_{ii}$ and $V=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n-1}\Bbbk e_{i,i+1}$ as in Example~\ref{ex:tensor-An}.
There are no quadratic relations, so $A^!$ has the reversed generators $e_{i,i+1}^*\in e_{i+1,i+1}V^*e_{ii}$ and every composable product of two generators is zero.
Consequently $h_{A^!}(t)=n+(n-1)t$.
For $n=3$,
\[
(\Bbbk A_3)^!\cong\bigl(\Bbbk A_3/(e_{13})\bigr)^\op,
\qquad
\bigl(\Bbbk A_3/(e_{13})\bigr)^!\cong(\Bbbk A_3)^\op.
\]
These are graded isomorphisms fixing the diagonal idempotents.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:alternating-quadratic-dual}
Take $A=T_S(V)/(ab)$ from Example~\ref{ex:two-vertex-relations}, where $S=\Bbbk e_1\oplus\Bbbk e_2$, $e_1ae_2=a$, and $e_2be_1=b$.
The only composable words of length $2$ are $ab$ and $ba$. Thus
\[
A^!=T_S(V^*)/(a^*b^*),\qquad
A^!=S\oplus\Bbbk a^*\oplus\Bbbk b^*\oplus\Bbbk b^*a^*.
\]
The original relation $ab=0$ corresponds to the surviving word $b^*a^*$; the surviving word $ba$ corresponds to the dual relation $a^*b^*=0$.
In both algebras the Hilbert series is $2+2t+t^2$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Symmetric and exterior algebras}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:symmetric-exterior-dual}
For a finite-dimensional $\Bbbk$-vector space $V$,
\[
\Symm(V)^!\cong\bigwedge D(V),\qquad
\bigl(\bigwedge V\bigr)^!\cong\Symm(D(V)).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Here $S=\Bbbk$ and $V^*=D(V)$. Choose a basis $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ of $V$, with coordinate functionals $x_1^*,\ldots,x_n^*$.
The evaluation \eqref{eq:quadratic-pairings} reads
\[
\theta_r(x_j^*\otimes x_i^*)(x_p\otimes x_q)=\delta_{ip}\delta_{jq}.
\]
For $F=\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}x_i^*\otimes x_j^*$, vanishing on each $x_p\otimes x_q-x_q\otimes x_p$ is equivalent to $c_{pq}=c_{qp}$.
Thus the orthogonal complement of the symmetric relations is spanned by $(x_i^*)^2$ and $x_i^*x_j^*+x_j^*x_i^*$ for $i<j$, which are the exterior relations.
Vanishing on the exterior relations instead gives $c_{ii}=0$ and $c_{pq}+c_{qp}=0$. The orthogonal complement is therefore spanned by $x_i^*x_j^*-x_j^*x_i^*$ for $i<j$.
Now use the presentations in \eqref{eq:polynomial-exterior}.
\end{proof}

The proof works in characteristic $2$: the relations $(x_i^*)^2=0$ remain part of the exterior presentation even when the signs $+$ and $-$ coincide.
Example~\ref{ex:symmetric-orthogonals} is the calculation for two generators.

\subsection{Direct sums}

For graded algebras $A,B$, the finite direct sum $A\oplus B$ has componentwise multiplication, unit $(1_A,1_B)$, and $(A\oplus B)_i=A_i\oplus B_i$.
In particular, its degree zero algebra is $A_0\oplus B_0$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:dual-direct-sum}
Let $A=T_S(V)/(R)$ and $B=T_T(W)/(Q)$ be quadratic algebras. Then $A\oplus B$ is quadratic over $S\oplus T$, and
\[
(A\oplus B)^!\cong A^!\oplus B^!.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Regard $V$ and $W$ as $(S\oplus T)$-bimodules supported on the two respective factors.
Mixed tensors vanish, so
\[
\begin{aligned}
T_{S\oplus T}(V\oplus W)&\cong T_S(V)\oplus T_T(W),\\
(V\oplus W)\otimes_{S\oplus T}(V\oplus W)
&\cong(V\otimes_S V)\oplus(W\otimes_T W).
\end{aligned}
\]
The relations are $R\oplus Q$. Right module homomorphisms also split over the two factors, and their annihilator is $R^\perp\oplus Q^\perp$.
Apply Definition~\ref{def:quadratic-duals}.
\end{proof}

For example, $(\Bbbk A_2\oplus\Bbbk A_2)^!\cong(\Bbbk A_2)^\op\oplus(\Bbbk A_2)^\op$. Both degree zero algebras are $\Bbbk^{\oplus4}$; there are no products connecting the two summands.

\subsection{Tensor products and signs}

\begin{definition}\label{def:algebra-tensor-products}
The \defn{ordinary tensor product} of graded $\Bbbk$-algebras $A,B$ has underlying vector space $A\otimes_\Bbbk B$, the total grading of Definition~\ref{def:total-tensor-grading}, and multiplication
\[
(a\otimes b)(a'\otimes b')=aa'\otimes bb'.
\]
The \defn{graded tensor product} $A\otimes_\Bbbk^{\mathrm{gr}}B$ has the same graded vector space, with multiplication
\begin{equation}\label{eq:signed-tensor-product}
(a\otimes b)(a'\otimes b')=(-1)^{\deg b\,\deg a'}aa'\otimes bb'
\quad\text{for homogeneous }a,a',b,b'.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

In the ordinary tensor product, $a\otimes1$ and $1\otimes b$ commute. In the graded tensor product they satisfy
\[
(1\otimes b)(a\otimes1)=(-1)^{\deg a\,\deg b}(a\otimes1)(1\otimes b).
\]
The sign does not change the underlying graded vector space, so both tensor products have Hilbert series $h_A(t)h_B(t)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:hilbert-tensor}.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:dual-tensor-products}
Let $A=T_S(V)/(R)$ and $B=T_T(W)/(Q)$ be quadratic algebras, and assume that $S\otimes_\Bbbk T$ is semisimple.
Then both tensor products are quadratic over $S\otimes_\Bbbk T$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dual-tensor-products}
(A\otimes_\Bbbk B)^!\cong A^!\otimes_\Bbbk^{\mathrm{gr}}B^!,\qquad
(A\otimes_\Bbbk^{\mathrm{gr}}B)^!\cong A^!\otimes_\Bbbk B^!.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

The assumption on $S\otimes_\Bbbk T$ holds, for example, when both $S$ and $T$ are products of matrix algebras over $\Bbbk$. Over a general field it does not follow from semisimplicity of $S$ and $T$ alone.

\begin{proof}
The degree $1$ part of $A\otimes_\Bbbk B$ is $(V\otimes_\Bbbk T)\oplus(S\otimes_\Bbbk W)$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:tensor-properties}, its tensor square over $S\otimes_\Bbbk T$ decomposes into the four summands
\[
(V\otimes_S V)\otimes_\Bbbk T
\ \oplus\ \underbrace{V\otimes_\Bbbk W}_{\text{order }VW}
\ \oplus\ \underbrace{V\otimes_\Bbbk W}_{\text{order }WV}
\ \oplus\ S\otimes_\Bbbk(W\otimes_T W).
\]
Its quadratic relations consist of $R\otimes_\Bbbk T$, $S\otimes_\Bbbk Q$, and the mixed relations
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixed-tensor-relations}
(v\otimes1)(1\otimes w)-(1\otimes w)(v\otimes1)
\quad(v\in V,\ w\in W).
\end{equation}
Here the products are taken in the tensor algebra on the degree $1$ part, and their span is an $(S\otimes_\Bbbk T)$-sub-bimodule.
Let $C$ be the quotient of that tensor algebra by the three specified relation spaces.
The generator maps induce algebra homomorphisms $A\to C$, $a\mapsto\overline a$, and $B\to C$, $b\mapsto\overline b$.
Their images commute: the degree $0$ actions already commute across the two factors, and \eqref{eq:mixed-tensor-relations} makes the degree $1$ generators commute.
Consequently $A\otimes_\Bbbk B\to C$, $a\otimes b\mapsto\overline a\,\overline b$, is an algebra homomorphism.
It is inverse to the canonical homomorphism $C\to A\otimes_\Bbbk B$, since both composites fix the generating copies of $S,T,V,W$. Thus the stated relations give exactly $A\otimes_\Bbbk B$.
For the graded tensor product, the same construction uses $\overline b\,\overline a=(-1)^{\deg a\deg b}\overline a\,\overline b$ for homogeneous $a,b$.

The right $(S\otimes_\Bbbk T)$-duals of the two generator summands are $V^*\otimes_\Bbbk T$ and $S\otimes_\Bbbk W^*$, with evaluations
\[
(f\otimes t)(v\otimes t')=f(v)\otimes tt',\qquad
(s\otimes g)(s'\otimes w)=ss'\otimes g(w).
\]
These identifications hold for finite free right modules and hence for their direct summands.
The reversed tensor evaluation pairs each pure summand with its corresponding dual summand and exchanges the two mixed summands; all other pairings are zero.
The pure relation spaces have orthogonal complements $R^\perp\otimes_\Bbbk T$ and $S\otimes_\Bbbk Q^\perp$ in the corresponding pure summands.
The mixed relation space is the set of pairs $(z,-z)$ in the two copies of $V\otimes_\Bbbk W$.
For mixed tensors, the two reversed evaluations of $f\in V^*$ and $g\in W^*$ both give $f(v)\otimes g(w)$ on the corresponding copy.
Thus an annihilating pair of functionals must have equal components. As these simple tensors span the full dual, the mixed annihilator is spanned by
\[
(f\otimes1)(1\otimes g)+(1\otimes g)(f\otimes1)
\quad(f\in V^*,\ g\in W^*).
\]
These are the mixed relations of $A^!\otimes_\Bbbk^{\mathrm{gr}}B^!$, proving the first isomorphism in \eqref{eq:dual-tensor-products}.
For $A\otimes_\Bbbk^{\mathrm{gr}}B$, the minus sign in \eqref{eq:mixed-tensor-relations} is a plus sign. Its annihilator has a minus sign, giving the ordinary tensor product on the dual side.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:commuting-square-dual}
Take $A=\Bbbk A_2\otimes_\Bbbk\Bbbk A_2$.
Its degree zero algebra has basis $e_{ii}\otimes e_{jj}$ for $i,j\in\{1,2\}$, and its degree $1$ part has basis
\[
a=e_{12}\otimes e_{11},\quad
b=e_{22}\otimes e_{12},\quad
c=e_{11}\otimes e_{12},\quad
d=e_{12}\otimes e_{22}.
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:idempotent-tensors}, the tensor square of the degree $1$ part has basis $ab,cd$, and both map to $e_{12}\otimes e_{12}$. Thus the relation space is $\Bbbk(ab-cd)$.
Using the reversed generators from \eqref{eq:dual-basis-idempotents}, its orthogonal complement is $\Bbbk(b^*a^*+d^*c^*)$.
Hence the dual presentation has an anticommuting relation:
\[
ab=cd\text{ in }A,\qquad
b^*a^*=-d^*c^*\text{ in }A^!,\qquad
h_A(t)=h_{A^!}(t)=4+4t+t^2.
\]
The signs agree with Proposition~\ref{prop:dual-tensor-products}; in characteristic $2$, the same presentations remain valid with $-1=1$.
Here replacing $d^*$ by $-d^*$ removes the minus sign. In this example the sign distinguishes the specified presentations, not the isomorphism classes of algebras.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:essential-tensor-sign}
For $V=\Bbbk x\oplus\Bbbk y$, Proposition~\ref{prop:dual-tensor-products} gives
\[
\Symm(V)^!
\cong\bigl(\Bbbk[x]\otimes_\Bbbk\Bbbk[y]\bigr)^!
\cong\frac{\Bbbk[x^*]}{((x^*)^2)}\otimes_\Bbbk^{\mathrm{gr}}
       \frac{\Bbbk[y^*]}{((y^*)^2)}
\cong\bigwedge D(V).
\]
If $\Char\Bbbk\neq2$, the ordinary tensor product of the two square-zero algebras is commutative, whereas the graded tensor product is not: $x^*y^*=-y^*x^*\neq0$.
They are therefore not isomorphic. This sign cannot be removed by a change of generators.
\end{example}

\subsection{Dimensions and the later homological calculation}

Equality of Hilbert series in Examples~\ref{ex:alternating-quadratic-dual} and~\ref{ex:commuting-square-dual} is not a general property of quadratic duality; the symmetric and exterior series in Example~\ref{ex:hilbert-polynomial-exterior} already differ.
With several idempotents, it is useful to retain the dimensions of the individual blocks.

\begin{definition}\label{def:matrix-hilbert}
For a locally finite graded algebra with $A_0=\bigoplus_{i=1}^r\Bbbk e_i$, its \defn{matrix Hilbert series} is
\[
H_A(t)_{ij}:=\sum_{d\geq0}\dim_\Bbbk(e_iA_de_j)t^d
\quad(1\leq i,j\leq r).
\]
\end{definition}

The scalar series $h_A(t)$ is the sum of all entries of $H_A(t)$.
The matrix version remembers the left and right actions: \eqref{eq:idempotent-tensor-decomposition} turns tensor products over $A_0$ into matrix multiplication, while $S$-duality transposes the indices.
For $A=\Bbbk A_2$, for example,
\[
H_A(t)=\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
H_{A^!}(-t)^{\mathsf T}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-t\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
H_A(t)H_{A^!}(-t)^{\mathsf T}=I_2.
\]
In contrast, $h_A(t)h_{A^!}(-t)=4-t^2$, not $1$.

The condition called \emph{Koszul} will be defined in Lecture~5 using graded projective resolutions of $S$.
For such algebras, Lecture~8 will prove the identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matrix-koszul-preview}
H_A(t)H_{A^!}(-t)^{\mathsf T}=I_r
\qquad\text{if }A_0=\bigoplus_{i=1}^r\Bbbk e_i;
\end{equation}
see \cite[\S2.11]{BGS96}. It is not asserted for every quadratic algebra.
For the polynomial and exterior pair, the scalar instance is $(1-t)^{-n}(1-t)^n=1$.

The purpose of constructing $A^!$ is homological: for a Koszul algebra, its degree $i$ part computes the space $\Ext_A^i(S,S)$ of extensions, introduced in Lecture~6, and multiplication corresponds to joining extensions.
With right modules and composition taking the right factor first, Lecture~7 will identify the resulting algebra $\bigoplus_{i\geq0}\Ext_A^i(S,S)$ with the right quadratic dual $A^!$ over $S$.
The choice of right $S$-dual is reflected already in degree $1$, where $A_1^*=\Hom_S(A_1,S)$ appears. No homological claim is needed for the calculations in this lecture.
