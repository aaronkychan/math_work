\subsection{Free and projective modules}

Until Subsection~\ref{subsec:graded-resolutions}, $A$ is an ordinary $\Bbbk$-algebra and all modules are right $A$-modules.

\begin{definition}\label{def:free-module}
A module $F$ is \defn{free} with basis $\{u_i\mid i\in I\}$ if every $v\in F$ has a unique expression $v=\sum_{i\in I}u_i a_i$, where $a_i\in A$ and only finitely many $a_i$ are non-zero.
Equivalently, $F\cong\bigoplus_{i\in I}A$, with $u_i$ corresponding to $1$ in the $i$th summand.
\end{definition}

A map from a free module is determined by arbitrary images of its basis elements.
In particular, a generating family $\{m_i\mid i\in I\}$ of $M$ gives a surjection
\[
\bigoplus_{i\in I}A\longrightarrow M,\qquad
(a_i)_{i\in I}\longmapsto\sum_{i\in I}m_i a_i.
\]
Every module is therefore a quotient of a free module. If $M$ is finitely generated, the free module can be chosen to be $A^{\oplus m}$ for some integer $m\geq0$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:split-sequence}
A short exact sequence $0\to L\xrightarrow{i}X\xrightarrow{q}Y\to0$ \defn{splits} if there is an $A$-linear map $s:Y\to X$ with $qs=\id_Y$.
The map $s$ is called a \defn{section} of $q$.
\end{definition}

Such a section gives $X=i(L)\oplus s(Y)$: write $x=(x-sq(x))+sq(x)$, and use $qs=\id_Y$ to check that $i(L)\cap s(Y)=0$.
Thus $(l,y)\mapsto i(l)+s(y)$ is an isomorphism $L\oplus Y\to X$ compatible with the inclusion and quotient maps.

\begin{definition}\label{def:projective-module}
A module $P$ is \defn{projective} if, for every surjective $A$-linear map $q:X\to Y$ and every $A$-linear map $f:P\to Y$, there is an $A$-linear map $\widetilde f:P\to X$ with $q\widetilde f=f$ (diagram commutes):
\[
\xymatrix@C=32pt@R=20pt{
& & P\ar[d]^f\ar@{-->}[dl]_{\exists \widetilde f}\\
\text{(exact row:)} & X\ar[r]_{
\forall q} & Y\ar[r] & 0 & .
}
\]
\end{definition}

Free modules have this lifting property: for each basis element $u_i$, choose $x_i\in X$ with $q(x_i)=f(u_i)$, and extend $u_i\mapsto x_i$ $A$-linearly.
Projective modules retain this property without requiring a basis.

For fixed $P$, postcomposition defines the functor $\Hom_A(P,-):\Mod A\to\Mod\Bbbk$, acting on $u:X\to Y$ by
\[
\Hom_A(P,u):\Hom_A(P,X)\longrightarrow\Hom_A(P,Y),
\qquad f\longmapsto u\circ f.
\]
Postcomposition preserves identities and composition.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:Hom-left-exact}
The functor $\Hom_A(P,-)$ is \defn{left exact}, that is, every short exact sequence $0\to L\xrightarrow{i}X\xrightarrow{q}Y\to0$ induces an exact sequence
\[
0\longrightarrow\Hom_A(P,L)\xrightarrow{i\circ-}
\Hom_A(P,X)\xrightarrow{q\circ-}\Hom_A(P,Y).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $i$ is injective, $i\circ f=0$ implies $f=0$.
For $g:P\to X$, the equality $qg=0$ is equivalent to $g(P)\subseteq\Ker q=i(L)$.
It is therefore equivalent to a unique factorisation $g=i\widetilde g$ with $\widetilde g:P\to L$ $A$-linear.
\end{proof}

Surjectivity of $q$ does not in general imply surjectivity of $q\circ-$.
Having a projective module is precisely the additional condition needed.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:projective-characterisations}
For a right $A$-module $P$, the following are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $P$ is projective.
\item The functor $\Hom_A(P,-)$ is exact.
\item Every surjective $A$-linear map $q:X\to P$ has an $A$-linear section.
\item $P$ is isomorphic to a direct summand of a free module.
\end{enumerate}
If $P$ is finitely generated, condition (4) can be replaced by $P\in\add A$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We prove $(1)\Leftrightarrow(2)$ and $(1)\Rightarrow(3)\Rightarrow(4)\Rightarrow(1)$.

\underline{$(1)\Leftrightarrow(2)$.}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:Hom-left-exact}, exactness is equivalent to surjectivity of
$q\circ-:\Hom_A(P,X)\to\Hom_A(P,Y)$ for every surjection $q:X\to Y$.
This says exactly that every $f:P\to Y$ lifts to $X$.

\underline{$(1)\Rightarrow(3)$.}
Apply the lifting property to $f=\id_P$.

\underline{$(3)\Rightarrow(4)$.}
Choose a free module $F$ and a surjection $q:F\to P$.
A section $s:P\to F$ gives $F=\Ker q\oplus s(P)$.
If $P$ is finitely generated, choose $F=A^{\oplus m}$.

\underline{$(4)\Rightarrow(1)$.}
Identify $P$ with a summand of a free module $F$, with inclusion $j:P\to F$ and projection $r:F\to P$ satisfying $rj=\id_P$.
For $q:X\to Y$ surjective and $f:P\to Y$, freeness gives $h:F\to X$ with $qh=fr$.
Then $\widetilde f=hj$ satisfies $q\widetilde f=frj=f$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:projective-sums}
Direct summands of projective modules are projective.
For an arbitrary family $(P_i)_{i\in I}$ of projective modules indexed by a set $I$, the module $\bigoplus_{i\in I}P_i$ is projective.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For a direct summand, use the inclusion and projection as in $(4)\Rightarrow(1)$ above.
A map from $\bigoplus_{i\in I}P_i$ is determined by its restrictions to the $P_i$; lift each restriction and combine the lifts.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:projective-idempotents}
For an idempotent $e\in A$, the decomposition $A=eA\oplus(1-e)A$ makes $eA$ projective.
Such modules need not be free: for $A=\Bbbk A_2$, the module $e_{22}A$ has dimension $1$, whereas every non-zero free module has dimension at least $\dim_\Bbbk A=3$.
\end{example}


If $A$ is finite-dimensional semisimple, every surjection splits by Theorem~\ref{thm:artin-wedderburn}; hence every $A$-module is projective.

\subsection{Tops and Nakayama's lemma}

The ideal $\rad A$ is two-sided: it consists precisely of the elements that act by zero on every simple right $A$-module.

\begin{supplementary}
For a simple module $L$ and $0\neq l\in L$, the map $A\to L$, $a\mapsto la$, is surjective with maximal right ideal as kernel.
Thus $l\rad A=0$.
Conversely, if $a\in A$ acts by zero on every simple module, apply this to $A/U$ for each maximal right ideal $U$ to obtain $a\in U$, hence $a\in \rad A$.
The identities $l(ba)=(lb)a=0$ and $l(ab)=(la)b=0$ for $a\in \rad A$, $b\in A$, and $l\in L$ show that $ba,ab\in \rad A$.
\end{supplementary}

For a finitely generated right $A$-module $M$, a set of elements generates $M$ if its images generate $M/M\rad A$.
This is the content of Nakayama's lemma.

\begin{lemma}[Nakayama's lemma]\label{lem:nakayama}
Let $M$ be a finitely generated right $A$-module.
If $M=M\rad A$, then $M=0$.
More generally, if $N\subseteq M$ is a submodule and $N+M\rad A=M$, then $N=M$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{supplementary}
\begin{proof}
For $a\in \rad A$, the right ideal $(1-a)A$ equals $A$.
Otherwise it is contained in a maximal right ideal $U$; then $a,1-a\in U$ gives $1\in U$, a contradiction.
Thus $(1-a)b=1$ for some $b\in A$.

Suppose $M=M\rad A\neq0$ and choose generators $m_1,\ldots,m_r$ with $r$ minimal.
Since $\rad A$ is a two-sided ideal, $M\rad A=\sum_{j=1}^r m_j\rad A$.
Write $m_r=\sum_{j=1}^r m_j a_j$ with $a_j\in \rad A$ and choose $b\in A$ with $(1-a_r)b=1$.
Then
\[
m_r(1-a_r)=\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}m_j a_j,
\qquad
m_r=\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}m_j a_j b,
\]
contradicting the minimality of $r$.
For the last assertion, apply the first to the finitely generated quotient $M/N$, which satisfies $(M/N)\rad A=M/N$.
\end{proof}
\end{supplementary}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:nakayama-finite-dimensional}
If $A$ is finite-dimensional, then $\rad A$ is \defn{nilpotent}: $\rad^r A := (\rad A)^r=0$ for some integer $r\geq1$.
Indeed, the powers stabilise at $\rad^r A=\rad^{r+1} A$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:nakayama} applied to the finite-dimensional module $(\rad A)^r$ gives $(\rad A)^r=0$.
Thus Nakayama's lemma applies even for infinitely generated modules: $M=\rad M$ implies $M=M\rad^r A=0$.
Applying this to $M/N$ also gives $N+\rad M=M\Longrightarrow N=M$ for every submodule $N\subseteq M$.
\end{remark}

For the rest of this subsection, assume that $A$ is finite-dimensional.
Every maximal right ideal of $A$ contains $\rad A$, so $\rad(A/\rad A)=0$; hence $A/\rad A$ is semisimple by Theorem~\ref{thm:artin-wedderburn}.

\begin{definition}\label{def:module-top}
Let $A$ be a finite-dimensional algebra.
The \defn{top} of a right $A$-module $M$ is
\[
\top M:=M/\rad M.
\]
\end{definition}

The module $\top M$ is semisimple because $\rad A$ acts by zero and $A/\rad A$ is semisimple.
Moreover, every map from $M$ to a semisimple module $T$ vanishes on $\rad M$, since $\rad T=0$, and therefore factors uniquely through $M\to\top M$.
Thus $\top M$ is the maximal semisimple quotient of $M$.

For an $A$-linear map $f:M\to N$, the inclusion $f(\rad M)\subseteq \rad N$ gives
\[
\top f:\top M\longrightarrow\top N,\qquad m+\rad M\longmapsto f(m)+\rad N.
\]
If $M$ is finitely generated, Nakayama's lemma says that lifts to $M$ of generators of $\top M$ generate $M$.
In particular, if $f:X\to M$ induces a surjection on tops, then $f(X)+\rad M=M$, so $f$ is surjective.

\begin{example}
For $A=\Bbbk A_2$, we have $\rad A=\Bbbk e_{12}$ and
\[
\top(e_{11}A)=e_{11}A/\Bbbk e_{12}=L_1,
\qquad \top(e_{22}A)=e_{22}A=L_2.
\]
\end{example}


\subsection{Projective covers}\label{subsec:ordinary-covers}

A free surjection onto $M$ may use unnecessary generators.
A projective cover is a surjection from a projective module whose domain cannot be replaced by a proper submodule still mapping onto $M$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:projective-cover}
Let $M$ be an $A$-module.  A \defn{projective cover} is a surjective map $p:P\to M$ with $P$ projective such that
\[
U\subseteq P\text{ a submodule and }p(U)=M
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad U=P.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:cover-top}
Let $A$ be finite-dimensional, let $P$ be a projective right $A$-module, and let $p:P\to M$ be surjective.
The following are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $p$ is a projective cover.
\item $\Ker p\subseteq \rad P$.
\item $\top p:\top P\to\top M$ is an isomorphism.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We prove $(1)\Rightarrow(2)\Rightarrow(1)$ and $(2)\Leftrightarrow(3)$.

\underline{$(1)\Rightarrow(2)$.}
If $\Ker p\not\subseteq\rad P$, there is a maximal submodule $U\subset P$ not containing $\Ker p$.
Then $U+\Ker p=P$, so $p(U)=M$, contradicting (1).

\underline{$(2)\Rightarrow(1)$.}
If $p(U)=M$, then $U+\Ker p=P$.
Condition (2) gives $U+\rad P=P$, hence $\rad(P/U)=P/U$.
Remark~\ref{rem:nakayama-finite-dimensional} gives $P/U=0$, so $U=P$.

\underline{$(2)\Leftrightarrow(3)$.}
The equality $p(\rad P)=\rad M$ makes $\top p$ surjective, with kernel
$(\Ker p+\rad P)/\rad P$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:ordinary-projective-covers}
Let $A$ be a finite-dimensional algebra.
Every right $A$-module $M$ has a projective cover; if $M$ is finite-dimensional, then so is its cover.
Over any algebra, for any two projective covers $p:P\to M$ and $q:Q\to M$, there is an isomorphism $f:P\to Q$ with $qf=p$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\underline{Uniqueness:}
Projectivity of $P$ gives $f:P\to Q$ with $qf=p$.
Since $q(f(P))=M$ and $q$ is a cover, $f(P)=Q$.
Projectivity of $Q$ now gives a section $s:Q\to P$ of $f$.
The equality $p(s(Q))=q(Q)=M$ and the cover property of $p$ give $s(Q)=P$.
Thus $f$ and $s$ are inverse isomorphisms.

\begin{supplementary}
\underline{Existence for finite-dimensional $M$:}
Choose a surjection $p:P\to M$ from a finite-dimensional projective module with $\dim_\Bbbk P$ as small as possible.
Such a surjection exists because $M$ is a quotient of some $A^{\oplus m}$.
Suppose $U\subsetneq P$ and $p(U)=M$.
Projectivity gives $h:P\to U$ with $(p|_U)h=p$.
Composing $h$ with the inclusion $U\hookrightarrow P$ gives $u:P\to P$ with $pu=p$ and $u(P)\subseteq U$.

Choose an integer $r\geq1$ such that the kernels and images of the powers of $u$ have stabilised.
Then
\[
P=\Ker u^r\oplus\Im u^r.
\]
Indeed, if $u^r(x)\in\Ker u^r$, then $x\in\Ker u^{2r}=\Ker u^r$, so $u^r(x)=0$; equality follows by the dimension formula.
Thus $\Im u^r$ is projective, and $pu^r=p$ shows that $p(\Im u^r)=M$.
But $\Im u^r\subseteq U\subsetneq P$ contradicts the minimal choice of $\dim_\Bbbk P$.
Therefore $p$ is a projective cover.

\underline{Existence for arbitrary $M$:}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:artin-wedderburn}, choose a decomposition
$\top M\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda\in I}L_\lambda$ into simple right $A/\rad A$-modules, indexed by a set $I$.
Each $L_\lambda$ is finite-dimensional, so the preceding argument gives a finite-dimensional projective cover $q_\lambda:Q_\lambda\to L_\lambda$.
Projectivity lifts each composite $Q_\lambda\to L_\lambda\hookrightarrow\top M$ through the quotient $M\to\top M$ to a map $f_\lambda:Q_\lambda\to M$.
Set
\[
P:=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in I}Q_\lambda,\qquad
p:P\longrightarrow M,\qquad
(x_\lambda)_{\lambda\in I}\longmapsto\sum_{\lambda\in I}f_\lambda(x_\lambda).
\]
The sum defining $p$ is finite for each element of $P$.
Corollary~\ref{cor:projective-sums} makes $P$ projective.
The equality $\rad P=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in I}\rad Q_\lambda$ gives $\top P\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda\in I}\top Q_\lambda$.
Thus the isomorphisms $\top q_\lambda$ identify $\top p$ with
$\bigoplus_{\lambda\in I}L_\lambda\xrightarrow{\sim}\top M$.
Thus $M=p(P)+\rad M$, and Remark~\ref{rem:nakayama-finite-dimensional} applied to $M/p(P)$ shows that $p$ is surjective.
Proposition~\ref{prop:cover-top} now makes $p$ a projective cover.
\end{supplementary}
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
For finite-dimensional $M$, finite generation gives a surjection $A^{\oplus m}\to M$, and finite-dimensionality of $A$ makes its domain finite-dimensional.
Finite-dimensionality of $P$ ensures that the kernels and images of powers of $u$ stabilise.
For arbitrary $M$, the argument instead uses semisimplicity of $A/\rad A$ and nilpotence of $\rad A$.
Uniqueness of projective covers requires no finiteness assumption.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}\label{ex:ordinary-A2-covers}
For $A=\Bbbk A_2$, the maps $e_{11}A\to L_1$ and $\id:e_{22}A\to L_2$ are projective covers: their kernels are respectively $\rad(e_{11}A)$ and $0$.
In contrast, the surjection $A=e_{11}A\oplus e_{22}A\to L_1$ obtained by killing $e_{22}A$ is not a cover, since its restriction to $e_{11}A$ is already surjective.
\end{example}

\subsection{Complexes and resolutions}

Starting with a surjection from a projective module onto $M$, one can repeat the construction for each successive kernel.
Composing each surjection with the inclusion of its target kernel gives an exact sequence.
We now define such resolutions over an arbitrary $\Bbbk$-algebra $A$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:chain-complex}\label{not:chain-differentials}
A \defn{chain complex} $C_\bullet$ of right $A$-modules is a sequence
\[
\cdots\longrightarrow C_{i+1}\xrightarrow{d_{i+1}}C_i
\xrightarrow{d_i}C_{i-1}\longrightarrow\cdots,
\qquad d_i d_{i+1}=0\quad\text{for all }i\in\Z.
\]
The maps $d_i$ are $A$-linear and are called \defn{differentials}; the index $i$ is the \defn{homological degree}.
Its \defn{homology} in degree $i$ is
\[
H_i(C_\bullet):=\Ker d_i/\Im d_{i+1}.
\]
\end{definition}

The identity $d_i d_{i+1}=0$ ensures that $\Im d_{i+1}\subseteq\Ker d_i$, so the quotient is defined.
Exactness at $C_i$ means $H_i(C_\bullet)=0$.
A complex concentrated in non-negative homological degrees has the form
$\cdots\to C_2\to C_1\to C_0\to0$; in particular, $H_0(C_\bullet)=C_0/\Im d_1$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:projective-resolution}
A \defn{projective resolution} of $M$ is an exact sequence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:projective-resolution}
\cdots\longrightarrow P_2\xrightarrow{d_2}P_1
\xrightarrow{d_1}P_0\xrightarrow{p_0}M\longrightarrow0
\end{equation}
with every $P_i$ projective.
We write $P_\bullet\to M$ for this data; the complex $P_\bullet$ itself ends in $P_0\to0$.
A resolution is \defn{free} if every $P_i$ is free.
\end{definition}

Thus $p_0$ is surjective, $\Ker p_0=\Im d_1$, and $\Ker d_i=\Im d_{i+1}$ for all $i\geq1$.
Equivalently, $p_0$ induces $H_0(P_\bullet)\cong M$, and $H_i(P_\bullet)=0$ for all $i\geq1$.
The module $M$ is not an additional term of $P_\bullet$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:resolutions-exist}
Every right $A$-module has a free resolution, hence a projective resolution.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For every right $A$-module $N$, the free module $A^{\oplus N}:=\bigoplus_{n\in N}A$, indexed by the underlying set of $N$, surjects onto $N$ by
\[
A^{\oplus N}\longrightarrow N,\qquad
(a_n)_{n\in N}\longmapsto\sum_{n\in N}na_n.
\]
Start with $N=M$ and repeat for each successive kernel.
Compose each new surjection with the inclusion of that kernel into the preceding free module; its image is exactly that kernel, so the resulting sequence is exact.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:ordinary-minimal-resolution}
A projective resolution \eqref{eq:projective-resolution} is \defn{minimal} if $p_0:P_0\to M$ and the surjections
\[
\begin{aligned}
p_1:P_1&\longrightarrow\Ker p_0,& x&\longmapsto d_1(x),\\
p_i:P_i&\longrightarrow\Ker d_{i-1},& x&\longmapsto d_i(x)
\quad\text{for all }i\geq2
\end{aligned}
\]
are projective covers.
\end{definition}

For the diagram, put $K_1:=\Ker p_0$ and $K_i:=\Ker d_{i-1}$ for all $i\geq2$.
In a minimal resolution, each differential factors as a projective cover onto its image followed by inclusion:
\[
\xymatrix@C=30pt@R=30pt{
\cdots\ar[r] & P_2\ar[rr]^{d_2}\ar@{->>}[dr]|{\substack{p_2\\\text{proj.\ cover}}}
& & P_1\ar[rr]^{d_1}\ar@{->>}[dr]|{\substack{p_1\\\text{proj.\ cover}}}
& & P_0\ar[rr]|{p_0}_{\text{proj.\ cover}} && M\ar[r] & 0\\
& & K_2\ar@{^{(}->}[ur] && K_1\ar@{^{(}->}[ur]
}
\]

When $A$ is finite-dimensional, Proposition~\ref{prop:cover-top} gives the criterion
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ordinary-minimal-criterion}
\Im d_i\subseteq \rad P_{i-1}\quad\text{for all }i\geq1.
\end{equation}
Indeed, exactness gives $\Ker p_0=\Im d_1$ and $\Ker d_i=\Im d_{i+1}$ for all $i\geq1$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:ordinary-minimal-resolutions}
Every right module over a finite-dimensional algebra has a minimal projective resolution.
If the module is finite-dimensional, every term of its minimal resolution is finite-dimensional.
Over any algebra, any two minimal resolutions $P_\bullet\to M$ and $P'_\bullet\to M$ are \defn{isomorphic}: there are isomorphisms $f_i:P_i\to P'_i$ satisfying
\[
p'_0f_0=p_0,\qquad
d'_i f_i=f_{i-1}d_i\quad\text{for all }i\geq1.
\]
\end{theorem}

More generally, maps $f_i$ satisfying these identities are called a \defn{map of resolutions}; the identities express the commutativity of the diagram:
\[
\xymatrix@C=24pt@R=22pt{
\cdots\ar[r] & P_1\ar[r]^{d_1}\ar[d]^{f_1}
& P_0\ar[r]^{p_0}\ar[d]^{f_0} & M\ar[r]\ar[d]^{\id_M} & 0\\
\cdots\ar[r] & P'_1\ar[r]^{d'_1}
& P'_0\ar[r]^{p'_0} & M\ar[r] & 0.
}
\]

\begin{proof}
Use projective covers in place of free surjections in Proposition~\ref{prop:resolutions-exist}.
For finite-dimensional $A$, Theorem~\ref{thm:ordinary-projective-covers} applies to every successive kernel.
If $M$ is finite-dimensional, choose a finite-dimensional cover at each step; its kernel is again finite-dimensional.
For uniqueness, choose an isomorphism $f_0:P_0\to P'_0$ between the covers of $M$.
It restricts to an isomorphism $\Ker p_0\to\Ker p'_0$; uniqueness of covers of these kernels gives $f_1:P_1\to P'_1$.
Repeating gives $f_i$ with $d'_i f_i=f_{i-1}d_i$ at every step, without finiteness assumptions.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
For a minimal projective resolution of $M$, the kernels $\Ker p_0$ and $\Ker d_i$ for $i\geq1$ are called the successive \defn{syzygies} of $M$.
Their isomorphism classes are independent of the chosen minimal resolution by Theorem~\ref{thm:ordinary-minimal-resolutions}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
Over an arbitrary algebra, Proposition~\ref{prop:resolutions-exist} still gives a free resolution, but projective covers of the successive kernels need not exist.
The obstruction is to existence, not to uniqueness of a minimal resolution.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}\label{ex:ordinary-A2-resolution}
The covers in Example~\ref{ex:ordinary-A2-covers} give a minimal resolution of $L_1$:
\[
0\longrightarrow e_{22}A\xrightarrow{d_1}e_{11}A
\xrightarrow{p_0}L_1\longrightarrow0,\qquad
d_1(e_{22}a)=e_{12}a.
\]
Indeed, $\Ker p_0=\Bbbk e_{12}$, and $d_1:e_{22}A\to\Bbbk e_{12}$ is an isomorphism.
Thus no further projective terms are needed.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:ordinary-exterior-resolution}
A finite-dimensional algebra can have a simple module with an infinite minimal resolution.
Let $E=\Bbbk[\epsilon]/(\epsilon^2)$ and $\pi:E\to\Bbbk$, $a+b\epsilon\mapsto a$.
Every $a+b\epsilon$ with $a\neq0$ has inverse $a^{-1}-a^{-2}b\epsilon$, so $\Bbbk\epsilon$ is the unique maximal ideal and $\rad E=\Bbbk\epsilon$.
The sequence
\[
\cdots\xrightarrow{\epsilon\cdot-}E\xrightarrow{\epsilon\cdot-}E
\xrightarrow{\epsilon\cdot-}E\xrightarrow{\pi}\Bbbk\longrightarrow0
\]
is a minimal resolution: multiplication by $\epsilon$ has kernel and image both equal to $\Bbbk\epsilon$.
At every step the kernel is non-zero, so the construction by projective covers never terminates.
\end{example}

\subsection{Graded version}\label{subsec:graded-resolutions}

Now let $A=\bigoplus_{d\in\Z}A_d$ be locally finite-dimensional non-negatively graded with $S=A_0$ finite-dimensional semisimple.
Recall that $A_+=\bigoplus_{d>0}A_d$ and $A/A_+\cong S$ so that we can regard $S$ as a graded right $A$-module concentrated in degree $0$.
All maps in this subsection are degree-preserving unless otherwise stated.

\begin{definition}\label{def:graded-projective}
A \defn{graded free module} is a direct sum $\bigoplus_{i\in I}A(-d_i)$ for some set $I$ and $d_i\in\Z$.
A graded module is \defn{graded projective} if it has the lifting property of Definition~\ref{def:projective-module} for degree-preserving maps.
\end{definition}

The generator $1$ of $A(-d)$ has degree $d$.
For a graded surjection $q:X\to Y$, an element of $Y_d$ has a preimage in $X_d$.
Thus the basis-lifting proof works for graded free modules.
Every graded module is a quotient of a graded free module, by taking homogeneous generators.
\begin{corollary}\label{cor:graded-projective-characterisations}
For a graded right $A$-module $P$, the following are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $P$ is graded projective.
\item The functor $\Hom_A^\Z(P,-)$ is exact on graded modules.
\item Every degree-preserving surjection onto $P$ has a degree-preserving section.
\item $P$ is a graded direct summand of a graded free module.
\end{enumerate}
In particular, $Q\otimes_S A(-d)$ is graded projective for every finite-dimensional right $S$-module $Q$ and every $d\in\Z$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
We follow $(1)\Leftrightarrow(2)$ and $(1)\Rightarrow(3)\Rightarrow(4)\Rightarrow(1)$ from Proposition~\ref{prop:projective-characterisations}, using degree-preserving maps.

\underline{$(1)\Leftrightarrow(2)$.} The proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:Hom-left-exact} applies to graded Hom; surjectivity on Hom is the graded lifting property.

\underline{$(1)\Rightarrow(3)$.} Lift $\id_P$.

\underline{$(3)\Rightarrow(4)$.} Split a graded free surjection onto $P$.

\underline{$(4)\Rightarrow(1)$.} Restrict lifts from the graded free module to its summand $P$.
The proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:projective-sums} also applies with degree-preserving maps.
For the last assertion, Corollary~\ref{cor:semisimple-summands} makes $Q$ a direct summand of $S^{\oplus m}$ for some $m$, so $Q\otimes_S A(-d)$ is a graded direct summand of $A(-d)^{\oplus m}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{not:graded-top}
The \defn{graded top} of a graded right $A$-module $M$ is the graded right $S$-module
\[
\grtop M:=M/MA_+.
\]
\end{definition}

For a degree-preserving map $f:M\to N$, write
$\grtop f:\grtop M\to\grtop N$, $m+MA_+\mapsto f(m)+NA_+$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:graded-radical}
If $A$ is finite-dimensional, then $A_+=\rad A$.
Consequently, $\grtop M=\top M$ for every graded right $A$-module $M$, with $\top M$ carrying the quotient grading.
\end{lemma}

\begin{supplementary}
\begin{proof}
If $A$ is finite-dimensional, then $A_d=0$ for all sufficiently large $d$, so $(A_+)^r=0$ for some integer $r\geq1$.
For any simple right module $L$, the submodule $LA_+$ is either $0$ or $L$.
The latter gives $L=L(A_+)^r=0$, a contradiction.
Thus $A_+$ annihilates every simple module and is contained in $\rad A$.
Since $A/A_+\cong S$ is semisimple, every non-zero element of $S$ acts non-trivially on some simple right $S$-module.
Regarding these as simple $A$-modules shows that $\rad A\subseteq A_+$.
\end{proof}
\end{supplementary}

The following exercise shows that Lemma~\ref{lem:graded-radical} fails for infinite-dimensional $A$, and that projective covers need not exist over such $A$.

\begin{exercise}
Let $A=\Bbbk[x]$ with $\deg x=1$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Prove that $\rad A=0$ whereas $A_+=(x)$.
In particular, $A/\rad A=A$, whereas $\grtop A=\Bbbk$.
\item Prove that the ordinary module $\Bbbk=A/(x)$ has no projective cover.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{hidden}
\emph{Solution.}
(1) If $0\neq f\in\rad A$, then $xf\in\rad A$, so $1-xf$ has a right inverse.
This is impossible because $1-xf$ has positive degree.
(2) Suppose $p:P\to\Bbbk$ is a projective cover, and choose $u\in P$ with $p(u)=1$.
Then $p(uA)=\Bbbk$, so $P=uA$.
The surjection $A\to P$, $a\mapsto ua$, splits because $P$ is projective.
The resulting projection $A\to A$ is multiplication by an idempotent $e\neq0$, and $P\cong eA$.
Since $\Bbbk[x]$ has no idempotents other than $0$ and $1$, we have $P\cong A$.
But every surjection $A\to\Bbbk$ maps the proper submodule $(x-1)A$ onto $\Bbbk$, contradicting the cover property.
\end{hidden}

Recall that a graded module $M$ is \defn{bounded below} if $M_{<b}=0$ for some $b$.
Every finitely generated graded module is bounded below: replace a finite generating set by its homogeneous components and take $b$ to be the least of their degrees.

\begin{lemma}[Graded Nakayama lemma]\label{lem:graded-nakayama}
For a bounded below graded right module $M$,
\[
\grtop M:=M/MA_+=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad M=0.
\]
If $N\subseteq M$ is a graded submodule and $N+MA_+=M$, then $N=M$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $M=MA_+\neq0$, choose the least integer $d$ with $M_d\neq0$.
But $(MA_+)_d=\sum_{r>0}M_{d-r}A_r=0$, a contradiction.
The second assertion follows by applying the first to $M/N$.
\end{proof}

Note that, in contrast to the ungraded bersion, graded Nakayama lemma only needs $M$ to be bounded below, without any finite generation assumption.
In particular, (the lifts of) homogeneous generators of $\grtop M$ generate $M$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:graded-projective-cover}
A \defn{graded projective cover} of a bounded below lfd graded module $M$ is a surjection $p:P\to M$, with $P$ bounded below, lfd, and graded projective, such that
\[
\grtop p:\grtop P\xrightarrow{\sim}\grtop M.
\]
\end{definition}

As in Proposition~\ref{prop:cover-top}, this condition is equivalent to $\Ker p\subseteq PA_+$, and to no proper graded submodule of $P$ mapping onto $M$.

\begin{supplementary}
The identity $p(PA_+)=MA_+$ gives $\Ker(\grtop p)=(\Ker p+PA_+)/PA_+$.
If $\Ker p\subseteq PA_+$ and $p(U)=M$ for a graded submodule $U\subseteq P$, then $U+PA_+=P$; graded Nakayama gives $U=P$.
Conversely, if $\Ker(\grtop p)\neq0$, choose a graded $S$-module complement $W$ to this kernel in $\grtop P$.
The inverse image $U$ of $W$ under $P\to\grtop P$ is a proper graded submodule of $P$.
Since $\grtop p(W)=\grtop M$, we have $p(U)+MA_+=M$; graded Nakayama gives $p(U)=M$.
\end{supplementary}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:graded-projective-covers}
Every bounded below lfd graded right $A$-module $M$ has a graded projective cover, unique up to a graded isomorphism.
Its domain is isomorphic to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:graded-cover-terms}
P=\bigoplus_{d\in\Z}\bigl((\grtop M)_d\otimes_S A\bigr)(-d),
\end{equation}
where $(\grtop M)_d$ is placed in degree $0$ before shifting.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $S$ is semisimple, choose right $S$-linear sections $s_d:(\grtop M)_d\to M_d$ of the quotient maps for every $d\in\Z$.
Define on each summand of \eqref{eq:graded-cover-terms}
\[
p:\bigl((\grtop M)_d\otimes_S A\bigr)(-d)\longrightarrow M,
\qquad v\otimes a\longmapsto s_d(v)a.
\]
The map is well-defined by $S$-linearity of $s_d$, and it preserves degree.
Each summand of $P$ is graded projective.
Its graded top is $(\grtop M)_d$ placed in degree $d$, since multiplication by $A_+$ generates precisely its components above degree $d$.
Thus $\grtop p$ identifies $\grtop P$ with $\grtop M$.
Graded Nakayama, applied to $M/p(P)$, makes $p$ surjective.
If $M_d=0$ for $d<b$, then $P_d=0$ for $d<b$, and
\[
P_q=\bigoplus_{b\leq d\leq q}(\grtop M)_d\otimes_S A_{q-d}
\]
is finite-dimensional for every $q\in\Z$.
Thus $p$ is a graded projective cover.
The uniqueness proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:ordinary-projective-covers} applies with degree-preserving lifts and sections.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:minimal-resolution}
A \defn{graded projective resolution} is a projective resolution \eqref{eq:projective-resolution} in which the modules are graded, the differentials are degree-preserving, and the $P_i$ are graded projective.
It is \defn{minimal} if $p_0:P_0\to M$ and the maps $p_i:P_i\to K_i$, $x\mapsto d_i(x)$, with $K_1=\Ker p_0$ and $K_i=\Ker d_{i-1}$ for $i\geq2$, are graded projective covers for all $i\geq1$.
\end{definition}

The homological degree $i$ and the grading degree $d$\comm{use another letter to not confuse with the differential.  I prefer using $h,k$ for homological degree and $i,j$ for internal/Adams grading} are distinct:
\[
d_i:(P_i)_d\longrightarrow(P_{i-1})_d.
\]
Exactness is checked separately in each grading degree, as in Remark~\ref{rem:graded-exactness}.
For bounded below locally finite-dimensional terms, minimality is equivalent to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:graded-minimal-criterion}
\Im d_i\subseteq P_{i-1}A_+\quad\text{for all }i\geq1.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:minimal-resolution}
Every bounded below locally finite-dimensional graded right $A$-module has a minimal graded projective resolution.
Any two such resolutions are isomorphic by degree-preserving maps commuting with the differentials and the maps to $M$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Repeat the construction and uniqueness proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:ordinary-minimal-resolutions}, using Proposition~\ref{prop:graded-projective-covers}.
Every kernel remains bounded below and locally finite-dimensional, so the construction continues at every step.
\end{proof}

For each term, \eqref{eq:graded-cover-terms} gives
\[
P_i\cong\bigoplus_{d\in\Z}\bigl((\grtop P_i)_d\otimes_S A\bigr)(-d).
\]
Thus the graded $S$-module $\grtop P_i$ records both the types and the degrees of the generators needed in homological degree $i$.


\begin{example}\label{ex:minimal-A2}
The ordinary resolution in Example~\ref{ex:ordinary-A2-resolution} becomes graded after a shift.
Let $A=\Bbbk A_2$ with $A_0=S=\Bbbk e_{11}\oplus\Bbbk e_{22}$ and $A_1=\Bbbk e_{12}$, and place $L_1,L_2$ in degree $0$.
Then
\[
0\longrightarrow e_{22}A(-1)\xrightarrow{d_1}e_{11}A
\xrightarrow{p_0}L_1\longrightarrow0,\qquad
d_1(e_{22}a)=e_{12}a
\]
is minimal: the generator $e_{22}$ of the shifted module and its image $e_{12}$ both have degree $1$, and $\Ker p_0=\Bbbk e_{12}=(e_{11}A)A_+$.
Its projective terms have tops $L_1$ and $L_2(-1)$.
The other simple module $L_2=e_{22}A$ is already projective.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:minimal-polynomial}
Let $A=\Bbbk[x]$ with $\deg x=1$.
The quotient $\Bbbk=A/(x)$ has the minimal graded resolution
\[
0\longrightarrow A(-1)\xrightarrow{d_1}A\xrightarrow{\pi}\Bbbk
\longrightarrow0,\qquad d_1(a)=xa,\quad\pi(f)=f(0).
\]
Multiplication by $x$ is injective, its image is $xA=\Ker\pi$, and the shift makes it degree-preserving.
The kernel of $\pi$ lies in $AA_+$, and the next kernel is zero.
The two projective terms therefore have one generator each, in degrees $0$ and $1$, respectively.
\end{example}

\begin{supplementary}
Forgetting the grading need not preserve projective covers.
In Example~\ref{ex:minimal-polynomial}, the proper ungraded submodule $(x-1)A\subset A$ still maps onto $\Bbbk$ under $\pi$, since $\pi(x-1)=-1$.
Thus $\pi$ is a graded projective cover but not an ordinary projective cover.
\end{supplementary}

\begin{example}\label{ex:minimal-exterior}
Give $E=\Bbbk[\epsilon]/(\epsilon^2)$ the grading $\deg\epsilon=1$.
The ordinary resolution in Example~\ref{ex:ordinary-exterior-resolution} becomes
\[
\cdots\longrightarrow E(-3)\xrightarrow{d_3}E(-2)
\xrightarrow{d_2}E(-1)\xrightarrow{d_1}E\xrightarrow{\pi}\Bbbk
\longrightarrow0,
\qquad d_i(a)=\epsilon a.
\]
For every $i\geq1$, the generator of $E(-i)$ and its image under $d_i$ both have degree $i$.
Exactness was checked in the ordinary resolution, and $\Im d_i=\epsilon E(-i+1)\subseteq E(-i+1)E_+$ proves minimality.
The term in homological degree $i$ has one generator, in degree $i$.
\end{example}

\begin{advanced}
\begin{remark}\label{not:graded-betti}
The multiplicities in the graded tops are sometimes called \defn{graded Betti numbers}.
For representatives $L_1,\ldots,L_n$ of the simple right $S$-modules, one can write
\[
(\grtop P_i)_d\cong\bigoplus_{j=1}^nL_j^{\oplus\beta_{i,d,j}(M)},
\qquad
\beta_{i,d}(M):=(\beta_{i,d,j}(M))_{1\leq j\leq n}.
\]
Here $i\geq0$ is the homological degree and $d\in\Z$ is the grading degree.
These multiplicities are independent of the chosen minimal resolution by Theorem~\ref{thm:minimal-resolution}.
We will use the modules $\grtop P_i$ directly, without this additional indexing.
\end{remark}
\end{advanced}

\begin{definition}\label{def:noetherian-algebra}
An algebra $A$ is \defn{right noetherian} if every submodule of every finitely generated right $A$-module is finitely generated.
It is \defn{left noetherian} if the same condition holds for left $A$-modules.
It is \defn{noetherian} if it is both right and left noetherian.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}\label{rem:noetherian-acc}
$A$ is right noetherian if and only if every ascending chain $I_1\subseteq I_2\subseteq\cdots$ of right ideals of $A$ stabilises, that is, if $A$ satisfies the \defn{ascending chain condition} (ACC) on right ideals; the analogous statement holds on the left.
Finite-dimensional algebras and $\Bbbk[x_1,\ldots,x_m]$ are noetherian, whereas the free algebra $\Bbbk\langle x,y\rangle$ is not.
Thus the hypothesis of Corollary~\ref{cor:noetherian-minimal-resolution} is not automatic.
\end{remark}

\begin{supplementary}
Suppose that every submodule of a finitely generated module is finitely generated.
Then the union of a chain $I_1\subseteq I_2\subseteq\cdots$ of right ideals is a finitely generated right ideal, and its generators lie in some $I_k$; so the chain stabilises at $I_k$.

Conversely, assume the ACC.
Every right ideal $I$ is finitely generated: otherwise choose inductively $u_{k+1}\in I\setminus(u_1A+\cdots+u_kA)$, which gives a strictly ascending chain.
We show by induction on $m$ that every submodule $U\subseteq A^{\oplus m}$ is finitely generated; the case $m=1$ was just proved.
For $m>1$, let $\pi:A^{\oplus m}\to A$ be the projection onto the last coordinate.
Then $U\cap\Ker\pi$ is a submodule of $A^{\oplus(m-1)}$ and $\pi(U)$ is a right ideal, so both are finitely generated.
Generators of $U\cap\Ker\pi$ together with lifts to $U$ of generators of $\pi(U)$ generate $U$.
Finally, a finitely generated module is a quotient $A^{\oplus m}/K$, and each submodule of it has the form $U/K$ with $K\subseteq U\subseteq A^{\oplus m}$, hence is finitely generated.
\end{supplementary}

If a graded module $M$ is finitely generated, then $\grtop M$ is finite-dimensional: the images of a finite homogeneous generating set generate $\grtop M$ over the finite-dimensional algebra $S$.
Its graded projective cover is therefore finitely generated by \eqref{eq:graded-cover-terms}.
The kernel need not be finitely generated unless we impose an additional condition.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:noetherian-minimal-resolution}
Assume that $A$ is right noetherian.
Every finitely generated graded right $A$-module has a minimal graded projective resolution whose terms and successive kernels are finitely generated.
In particular, this applies to $S=A/A_+$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
A finitely generated graded module is bounded below and locally finite-dimensional, since it is a quotient of a finite direct sum of shifts of $A$.
Its graded projective cover is finitely generated by the preceding paragraph.
Right noetherianity makes the kernel finitely generated, so the same argument applies successively to every kernel.
\end{proof}

Right noetherianity ensures finite generation at every step.
Without that assumption, boundedness below and local finite-dimensionality still suffice for existence of a minimal graded resolution by Theorem~\ref{thm:minimal-resolution}.
