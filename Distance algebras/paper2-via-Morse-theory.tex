\begin{abstract}
Let $L$ be a finite distributive lattice and let $\Sigma_L$ be the distance algebra of its Hasse graph.  For every indecomposable injective $\Sigma_L$-module, we construct a projective resolution  and use algebraic Morse theory to obtain a minimal resolution whose multiplicities are reduced Betti numbers of strict upset order complexes.  As a consequence, $\Sigma_L$ is (Iwanaga--)Gorenstein, with both self-injective dimensions bounded by the diameter of the Hasse graph of $L$.  We further prove that the first two terms of each minimal resolution are multiplicity-free, whereas arbitrary multiplicities occur in every higher projective degree. We show in the appendix that the non-minimal resolution constructed is actually a special case of simplicial bar construction that turns out to be exact everywhere.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Magnitude homology was introduced by Hepworth and Willerton as a categorification of the magnitude of a graph~\cite{HW17}. Its connection with homological algebra was made explicit by Asao and Ivanov, who realized magnitude homology as a derived functor over the distance algebra~\cite{AI24}. Asao and Wakatsuki subsequently constructed minimal projective resolutions for the distance algebras of geodetic metric spaces~\cite{AW24}. In this paper we study the distance algebras associated with the Hasse graphs of finite distributive lattices, a family in which shortest paths are generally not unique.

In our companion paper~\cite{CX26}, we studies broader representation-theoretic and homological properties of distance algebras of finite graphs. In particular, it shows that the distance algebra of a finite modular lattice is Auslander--Gorenstein precisely when the lattice is a finite direct product of chains. For a distributive lattice $L=\mathcal J(P)$, this is equivalent to every connected component of $P$ being a chain. The present paper establishes the Gorenstein and Iwanaga--Gorenstein properties for distance algebras of arbitrary finite distributive lattices under the corresponding assumptions on the coefficient ring.

By Birkhoff's fundamental theorem of distributive lattices, every finite distributive lattice has the form $\mathcal J(P)$ for a finite poset $P$. For the distance algebra $\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}$ over a commutative noetherian ring $R$, we construct a projective resolution of each module $I_X=D(\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)} e_X)$, indexed by strict chains in a symmetric-difference poset. These resolutions have length at most $|P|-1$. Together with the Gorenstein criterion of Iyengar--Krause~\cite[Theorem~4.6]{IK22}, this gives our first main result.

\begin{main}\label{main:minimal-resolution-and-ig}
\bhx{\textnormal{(see Corollary~\ref{cor:distributive-ig} and Proposition \ref{prop:n-2-inequalities})} Let $R$ be a commutative noetherian ring and let $P$ be a non-empty finite poset. Let $\mathcal J(P)$ be the lattice of order ideals of $P$, and let $\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}$ be the distance $R$-algebra of its Hasse graph.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $R$ is Gorenstein, then $\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}$ is a Gorenstein $R$-algebra.
\item If $R$ is Iwanaga--Gorenstein, then $\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}$ is Iwanaga--Gorenstein and
\[
 \idim(\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)})_{\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}}
 =\idim{}_{\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}}\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}
 \leq |P|-1+\idim_R R.
\]
\item If $R$ is Iwanaga--Gorenstein and $|P|\geq3$, then
\[
 \idim(\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)})_{\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}}
 =\idim{}_{\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}}\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}
 \leq |P|-2+\idim_R R.
\]
\end{enumerate}}
\end{main}

Over a field $R=\Bbbk$, we use algebraic Morse theory~\cite{CLZ26} to obtain minimal projective resolutions of the indecomposable injectives. Their multiplicities and lengths are given by the reduced homology of the strict upset order complexes from Subsection~\ref{subsec:strict-upsets}; see Theorem~\ref{thm:minimal-resolution} and Corollary~\ref{cor:SigmaJ(P) is IG}. This also computes the self-injective dimension of $\Sigma_L$.

The combinatorics of order ideals forces multiplicity-freeness in the first two terms. For higher degrees, height-two posets identify the relevant order complexes with barycentric subdivisions of independence complexes of bipartite graphs. A realization theorem of Nagel and Reiner~\cite{NR09} then produces arbitrary multiplicities.

\begin{main}\label{main:minimal-resolution-and-multiplicities}\textnormal{(see Theorem~\ref{thm:minimal-resolution}, Proposition~\ref{prop:low-degree-multiplicity}, and Corollary~\ref{cor:arbitrary-higher-multiplicities})}
For every finite poset $P$ and every $X\in\mathcal J(P)$, we construct a minimal projective resolution of the indecomposable injective corresponding to $X$. These resolutions have the following properties.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The first two terms are multiplicity-free.
\item Multiplicities can be arbitrarily large in every higher projective degree.
\end{enumerate}
\end{main}

\subsection{Duality and Gorenstein algebras}
\label{subsec:duality-gorenstein}

Let $A$ be a finite projective $R$-algebra.

Recall that a ring $B$ is \defn{Iwanaga--Gorenstein} if it is noetherian on both sides and
\[
 \idim B_B<\infty
 \qquad\text{and}\qquad
 \idim {}_B B<\infty.
\]
A finite projective $R$-algebra $A$ is called a \defn{Gorenstein $R$-algebra} if $A_{\mathfrak p}$ is Iwanaga--Gorenstein for every $\mathfrak p\in\operatorname{Spec}R$ such that $A_{\mathfrak p}\neq0$; see \cite[Definition~1.1]{IK22} and \cite[Definition~6.1]{GIK23}. When $R=\Bbbk$, this is precisely the Iwanaga--Gorenstein property.
Following Buchweitz \cite[Section~7.6]{Buc21}, the \defn{dualizing bimodule} of $A$ is
\[
 D(A):=\Hom_R(A,R) \text{ with action } (a f b)(c):=f(bca)
 \quad(a,b,c\in A,\ f\in D(A)).
\]
When $R$ is Gorenstein, a criterion of Iyengar--Krause \cite[Theorem~4.6]{IK22} (see also \cite[Theorem~6.7]{GIK23}) states that $A$ is a Gorenstein $R$-algebra if and only if
\begin{equation}
 \pdim_A D(A)<\infty
 \qquad\text{and}\qquad
 \pdim_{A^{\operatorname{op}}}D(A)<\infty;
 \label{eq:canonical-finite-pdim}
\end{equation}
equivalently, $D(A)$ admits a bounded resolution by finitely generated projective modules on each side.  For the distance algebras of finite distributive lattices, we will prove condition \eqref{eq:canonical-finite-pdim} without assuming that $R$ is Gorenstein.  When $R$ is Gorenstein, the above criterion then shows that these are Gorenstein $R$-algebras.

An \defn{$A$-lattice} is a right $A$-module whose underlying $R$-module is finitely generated and projective. We write $\lat(A)$ for the category of $A$-lattices, equipped with the exact structure inherited from the category of $A$-modules. Every short exact sequence in this category splits over $R$.

For finite projective algebras over a commutative noetherian ring, we recall \old{some facts} \bhx{the following fact} \comment{some facts? following fact?} from the theory of lattices over orders; see, for example, \cite[Section~25]{CR81}.
The following formulation can be found in \cite[Section~2]{BBIKP25}; we include a proof as it is straightforward.

\begin{lem}\label{lem:lattice-canonical-duality}
The functor $D$ induces an exact duality
\[
 D:\lat(A)^{\operatorname{op}}
 \xrightarrow{\sim}\lat(A^{\operatorname{op}}).
\]
The category $\lat(A)$ has enough projective and injective objects, given by $\add A$ and $\add D(A)$, respectively. Moreover, the projective dimension of an $A$-lattice in $\lat(A)$ equals its ordinary projective dimension.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
The functor $D$ is exact on $R$-split short exact sequences, and evaluation gives a natural isomorphism $M\xrightarrow{\sim}D^2(M)$ for every $M\in\lat(A)$. This proves the duality assertion.

For $M\in\lat(A)$, choose a surjection $A^{\oplus m}\twoheadrightarrow M$ with $m$ finite. It splits over $R$, so its kernel is again an $A$-lattice. Thus $\lat(A)$ has enough projectives, and its projective objects are precisely $\add A$. Assertion about enough injective follows by applying $D$ to left projective modules.  Since syzygy of $M\in \lat(A)$ stays in $\lat(A)$, projective resolution in $\lat(A)$ is the same as ordinary projective resolution; this last assertion follows.
\end{proof}

We call the objects of $\add D(A)$ \defn{relative injective} $A$-modules. For $M\in\lat(A)$, write $\idim^{\lat}_A M$ for its  injective dimension in $\lat(A)$, namely, the least length of a coresolution by the relative injectives, or $\infty$ if no finite such coresolution exists. When $R=\Bbbk$, relative injectives are the finitely generated ordinary injectives, and relative injective dimension agrees with ordinary injective dimension.
We say that $A$ is \defn{self-injective} if $A\cong D(A)$ as right $A$-module; note that in this case they are also isomorphic as left $A$-module and so $A$ is Iwanaga--Gorenstein.

\begin{cor}\label{cor:relative-ordinary-injective-dimension}
Let $A$ be a finite projective $R$-algebra. Then
\[
 \idim^{\lat}_A A
 =\pdim_{A^{\operatorname{op}}}D(A),
 \qquad
 \idim^{\lat}_{A^{\operatorname{op}}}A
 =\pdim_A D(A).
\]
If $\idim_R R<\infty$, then
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \idim A_A
 &\leq \idim^{\lat}_A A+\idim_R R
 =\pdim_{A^{\operatorname{op}}}D(A)+\idim_R R,\\
 \idim {}_A A
 &\leq \idim^{\lat}_{A^{\operatorname{op}}}A+\idim_R R
 =\pdim_A D(A)+\idim_R R.
\end{aligned}
\]
In particular, if $\idim_R R$, $\pdim_{A^{\operatorname{op}}}D(A)$, and $\pdim_{A}D(A)$ are all finite, then $A$ is Iwanaga--Gorenstein. 
When $R=\Bbbk$ is a field, both inequalities are equalities.
\end{cor}
%\comment{c.f. O. Iyama, M. Kalck, M. Wemyss and D. Yang, Frobenius categories, Gorenstein algebras and rational surface singularities, Compositio Mathematica 151 (2015), no. 3, 502–534, Lemma 4.17}

\begin{rmk}
For related formulas for self-injective dimensions, see \cite[Proposition~1.1(3)]{GN01} and \cite[Lemma~4.17]{IKWY15}. We include an elementary proof of the above estimate for completeness.
\end{rmk}

Then the quoted Lemma can be applied. (Dedekind domain is also the setting in Curtis--Reiner, which Iyama recommended using} \xnew{I see, thank you for the clarification.}

\begin{proof}
Since $A$ is finitely generated over the noetherian ring $R$, it is noetherian on both sides. By Lemma~\ref{lem:lattice-canonical-duality}, the exact duality $D:\lat(A)^{\operatorname{op}}
 \xrightarrow{\sim}\lat(A^{\operatorname{op}})$
swaps relative injective coresolutions of $A_A$ with projective resolutions of ${}_A D(A)$. Since projective dimension in the lattice category agrees with ordinary projective dimension, we obtain $\idim^{\lat}_A A
 =\pdim_{A^{\operatorname{op}}}D(A).$
Applying the same argument to $A^{\operatorname{op}}$ gives the other relative dimension equality.

Assume now that $\idim_R R<\infty$. 
We claim that $\idim D(A)_A\leq\idim_R R$.  Indeed, for any right $A$-module $N$, so long as $A$ is $R$-projective, any $A$-projective resolution of $N$ is also an $R$-projective resolution.  So tensor-Hom adjunction $\Hom_A(-,D(A)_A)\cong\Hom_R(-,R)$ gives
\[
 \Ext_A^q(N,D(A)_A)
 \cong \Ext_R^q(N,R)
 \qquad(q\geq0),
\]
and the claim follows.

Put $n:=\pdim_{A^{\operatorname{op}}}D(A)$. The bound for $\idim A_A$ is immediate if $n=\infty$. If $n<\infty$, choose a resolution by finitely generated projective left $A$-modules
\[
 0\longrightarrow Q_n\longrightarrow\cdots
 \longrightarrow Q_0\longrightarrow {}_A D(A)
 \longrightarrow0.
\]
The modules $Q_i$ and $D(A)$ are projective over $R$. Starting with the surjection $Q_0\to D(A)$, it follows inductively that every syzygy is projective over $R$ and every successive short exact sequence splits over $R$. Applying $D$ and using $D^2(A)\cong A$ then yields an exact sequence of right $A$-modules
\[
 0\longrightarrow A_A\longrightarrow D(Q_0)
 \longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow D(Q_n)
 \longrightarrow0.
\]
For every $0\leq i\leq n$, we have $D(Q_i)\in\add D(A)_A$, and hence $\idim_A D(Q_i)\leq\idim_R R$ by the claim in the previous paragraph. Dimension shifting along this exact sequence yields
\[
 \idim A_A
 \leq
 \max_{0\leq i\leq n}
 \bigl\{\idim_A D(Q_i)+i\bigr\}
 \leq \idim_R R+n.
\]
Applying the same argument to $A^{\operatorname{op}}$ gives
$\idim {}_A A\leq\idim_R R+\pdim_A D(A)$.  Thus, if both $\pdim_{A^{\operatorname{op}}}D(A)$ and $\pdim_{A}D(A)$ are finite, $A$ is Iwanaga--Gorenstein.

Finally, when $R=\Bbbk$, we have $\idim_R R=0$, and relative injective dimension agrees with ordinary injective dimension.  Putting this into the inequalities and the claim immediately follows.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Distance algebra}

Let $G=(V,E)$ be a connected combinatorial graph, where $V$ is the set of vertices and $E$ is the set of edges. In this paper, we assume that $G$ contains neither loops nor multiple edges.

For vertices $x,y\in V$, we write $\{x,y\}\in E$ if $x$ and $y$ are connected by an edge; in this case, they are called \defn{adjacent}. The \defn{distance} between $x$ and $y$, denoted by $$d(x,y)=d_G(x,y),$$ is the length of a shortest path from $x$ to $y$ on $G$, and the \defn{diameter} of $G$ is the maximal distance in $G$, denoted by $$\operatorname{diam}(G) :=\max_{x,y\in V}d(x,y).$$

\begin{dfn}
Let $G=(V,E)$ be a graph (without multi-edge and loops), and $Q_G$ be its \defn{double quiver}, i.e. the set of vertices of $Q_G$ is $V$ and there is a pair of arrows
\[\begin{tikzcd}
	x \arrow[r, "{a_{xy}}", bend left] & y \arrow[l, "{a_{yx}}", bend left]
\end{tikzcd} \text{ for each edge }\{x,y\}\in E.\]
The \defn{distance $R$-algebra} of $G$ is defined to be $\Sigma_G:=R Q_G/I_G$, where $I_G$ is the ideal generated by the following relations:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $p=0$, for every path $p$ such that $\ell(p)>d(s(p),t(p))$;
\item $p=q$, for every pair of paths $p,q$ in $Q_G$ with
$$s(p)=s(q),\qquad t(p)=t(q),\qquad
\ell(p)=d(s(p),t(p))=\ell(q).$$
\end{enumerate}
\end{dfn}

By definition, in $\Sigma_G$, a path is non-zero if and only if it is represented by a shortest path in $G$, and any two shortest paths with the same starting and ending vertices are identified. Hence, for any two vertices $x,y\in V$, there is a unique non-zero path from $x$ to $y$ modulo $I_G$. In particular, $e_x\Sigma_Ge_y$ is a free $R$-module of rank one for all $x,y\in V$, and $\Sigma_G$ is free of rank $|V|^2$ over $R$. When $R=\Bbbk$, the algebra $\Sigma_G$ is Schurian.

We take an $R$-basis $\{b_{xy}\mid x,y\in V\}$ for $\Sigma_G$, where $b_{xy}$ denotes the equivalence class of any shortest path from $x$ to $y$ on $G$, with the convention that $b_{xx}=e_x$ is the vertex idempotent associated to $x$. Then multiplication in $\Sigma_G$ is given by
$$
b_{xy}b_{yz}=
\begin{cases}
b_{xz}, & \text{if } d(x,z)=d(x,y)+d(y,z),\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$
Note that $b_{xy}$ is denoted by $(x,y)$ and by $e_{xy}$ in \cite{AI24} and \cite{AW24} respectively. Thus, each $\Sigma_G$ is an $R$-algebra.
Note that path length induces a natural grading on $\Sigma_G$; this matches the canonical (internal $\mathbb{R}$-)grading of magnitude homology. 

The graphs that we concern in this note are the Hasse diagrams of finite distributive lattices $L$.  We will simplify the notation and write $\Sigma_L$.



For each $x\in V(G)$, set
\[
 P_x:=e_x\Sigma_G,
 \qquad
 I_x:=D(\Sigma_G e_x).
\]
These are projective and relative injective right $\Sigma_G$-modules, respectively, and
\[
 (\Sigma_G)_{\Sigma_G}=\bigoplus_{x\in V(G)}P_x,
 \qquad
 D(\Sigma_G)_{\Sigma_G}=\bigoplus_{x\in V(G)}I_x.
\]
When $R=\Bbbk$, $P_x$ and $I_x$ are indecomposable, and $I_x$ is the injective envelope of $S_x:=P_x/\rad P_x$. \bhx{Moreover, every finitely generated projective right $\Sigma_G$-module $Q$ admits a decomposition $Q\cong\bigoplus_{x\in V(G)}P_x^{\oplus m_x}$, where the non-negative integers $m_x$ are uniquely determined by the Krull--Schmidt theorem. We call $m_x$ the \defn{multiplicity} of $P_x$ in $Q$ and write $[Q:P_x]:=m_x$. We call $Q$ \defn{multiplicity-free} if $[Q:P_x]\leq1$ for every $x\in V(G)$.}

Path reversal defines an $R$-linear anti-involution
\[
 \tau:\Sigma_G\longrightarrow\Sigma_G,
 \qquad b_{xy}\longmapsto b_{yx}.
\]
Thus $\tau$ induces an isomorphism of $R$-algebras
$\Sigma_G^{\operatorname{op}}\cong\Sigma_G$.

For a right $\Sigma_G$-module $M$, let $\tau_*M$ be the left
$\Sigma_G$-module with action $a\cdot m:=m\tau(a)$. This gives a covariant exact equivalence between right and left module categories, preserving projectives and injectives. Its inverse is defined by the same construction for left modules. Since $\tau(e_x)=e_x$, there is an isomorphism of left $\Sigma_G$-modules
\begin{equation}
 \tau_*I_x\xrightarrow{\sim}D(e_x\Sigma_G),
 \qquad f\longmapsto\bigl(b\longmapsto f(\tau(b))\bigr).
 \label{eq:tau-canonical-summands}
\end{equation}
Thus, we have the following lattice version of duality on distance algebras \cite[Section~2]{CX26}.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:distance-lattice-duality}
For $M\in\lat(\Sigma_G)$, equip $D(M)$ with the right $\Sigma_G$-action
\[
 (f\cdot a)(m):=f\bigl(m\tau(a)\bigr).
\]
With this right action, $D$ defines an exact contravariant self-duality of $\lat(\Sigma_G)$, with $ D(P_x)\cong I_x$ and $D(I_x)\cong P_x$. Consequently, for every $M\in\lat(\Sigma_G)$,
\[
 \idim^{\lat}_{\Sigma_G} M=\pdim_{\Sigma_G}D(M),
 \qquad
 \pdim_{\Sigma_G} M=\idim^{\lat}_{\Sigma_G}D(M).
\]
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Compose the duality of Lemma~\ref{lem:lattice-canonical-duality} with the equivalence induced by $\tau$. The asserted action and the isomorphisms follow from $\tau^2=\id$ and \eqref{eq:tau-canonical-summands}. This self-duality exchanges projective resolutions and relative injective coresolutions, giving the dimension equalities.
\end{proof}

Applying Corollary~\ref{cor:relative-ordinary-injective-dimension} to $\Sigma_G$ gives the following criterion. Here $D(\Sigma_G)$ is regarded as the dualizing bimodule with its natural left and right actions.

\begin{cor}\label{cor:distance-gorenstein-criterion}
Let $n:=\max_{x\in V(G)}\pdim_{\Sigma_G}I_x
 \in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}\cup\{\infty\}$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \pdim_{\Sigma_G}D(\Sigma_G)
 =\pdim_{\Sigma_G^{\operatorname{op}}}D(\Sigma_G)
 =\idim^{\lat}_{\Sigma_G}\Sigma_G
 =n.
 \label{eq:distance-canonical-dimensions}
\end{equation}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item If $R$ is Gorenstein, then $\Sigma_G$ is a Gorenstein
$R$-algebra if and only if $n<\infty$.
\item If $R$ is Iwanaga--Gorenstein and $n<\infty$, then $\Sigma_G$ is Iwanaga--Gorenstein and
\[
 \idim(\Sigma_G)_{\Sigma_G}
 =\idim{}_{\Sigma_G}\Sigma_G
 \leq n+\idim_R R.
\]
\item When $R=\Bbbk$ is a field, both self-injective dimensions equal $n$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{cor}

\begin{proof}
The decomposition $D(\Sigma_G)_{\Sigma_G}
 =\bigoplus_{x\in V(G)}I_x$ gives $\pdim_{\Sigma_G}D(\Sigma_G)=n$. Summing \eqref{eq:tau-canonical-summands} over the vertices gives an isomorphism of left $\Sigma_G$-modules
\[
 \tau_*\bigl(D(\Sigma_G)_{\Sigma_G}\bigr)
 \cong{}_{\Sigma_G}D(\Sigma_G).
\]
Since $\tau_*$ preserves projective dimensions, we also have
$\pdim_{\Sigma_G^{\operatorname{op}}}D(\Sigma_G)=n$. 
The remaining assertions follow immediately from Corollary~\ref{cor:relative-ordinary-injective-dimension}, the Gorenstein criterion \eqref{eq:canonical-finite-pdim}, and the equivalence induced by $\tau$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Simplicial complexes, homologies, and order complexes}
\label{subsec:simplicial-topology}

Unless a coefficient ring is specified, all chain groups and homology groups are taken over $R$.

A finite \defn{simplicial complex} $\Delta$ on a finite vertex set $V$ is a collection of subsets of $V$ such that $\sigma\in\Delta$ and $\tau\subseteq\sigma$ imply $\tau\in\Delta$. The elements of $\Delta$ are called its \defn{faces}, and a face $\sigma$ has \defn{dimension} $|\sigma|-1$. We denote by $\Delta_r$ the set of $r$-dimensional faces of $\Delta$.  We allow the void complex $\emptyset$, which has no faces. We fix a total order on the vertex set when necessary, then an $r$-dimensional face equipped with the the inherited ordering is called an \defn{$r$-chain} in $\Delta$.

For a finite poset $(P,<)$, its \defn{order complex} $\Delta(P)$ is the simplicial complex whose $r$-chains are the strict chains $x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_r$ in $P$. In particular, $\Delta(\emptyset)=\{\emptyset\}$ is not the void complex $\emptyset$. Note that we do not need to linearize $P$ to a total order since every $r$-simplex is already totally ordered by definition.

For $r\geq0$, let $C_r(\Delta):=R \Delta_r$ be the free $R$-module generated by the $r$-chains of $\Delta$. We denote the corresponding basis element by $\langle x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_r\rangle.$

For $r\geq1$, the simplicial \defn{boundary map} is given by
\[
\partial_r
\langle x_0<\cdots<x_r\rangle
 :=  \sum_{i=0}^{r}(-1)^i
 \langle x_0<\cdots<\widehat{x_i}<\cdots<x_r
 \rangle,
\]
where $\widehat{x_i}$ means that $x_i$ is removed from the chain. When we want to write in a more compact form, for a chain $x_\ast = ( x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_r )$, we write $x_\ast^{\widehat i}$ for the chain obtained from $x_\ast$ by deleting $x_i$; thus, we have $\partial_r \langle x_\ast\rangle =\sum_{i=0}^r (-1)^i \langle x_\ast^{\widehat{i}}\rangle$. The boundary maps satisfy $\partial_{r-1}\partial_r=0$. The \defn{ordinary simplicial homology} of $\Delta$ is the homology of the chain complex
\[
C_\bullet(\Delta)\colon
\quad
\cdots
\longrightarrow C_2(\Delta)
\xrightarrow{\partial_2}C_1(\Delta)
\xrightarrow{\partial_1}C_0(\Delta)
\longrightarrow0,
\]
and is denoted by $H_r(\Delta):=\Ker\partial_r/\Image\partial_{r+1}$.

Set $C_{-1}(\Delta):=R$ and define the \defn{augmentation map}
\[
\partial_0\colon C_0(\Delta)\longrightarrow C_{-1}(\Delta),
\qquad \partial_0\langle x\rangle:=1.
\]
Adjoining to $C_\bullet(\Delta)$ yields a chain complex starting in degree $-1$:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde C_\bullet(\Delta)\colon
\quad
\cdots
\longrightarrow C_1(\Delta)
\xrightarrow{\partial_1}C_0(\Delta)
\xrightarrow{\partial_0}C_{-1}(\Delta)
\longrightarrow0.
\label{eq:reduced-chain-complex}
\end{equation}
Its homology is the \defn{reduced homology} $\widetilde H_r(\Delta)$. In particular, we have
\[
\widetilde H_{-1}(\emptyset)=R,
\qquad \text{and}\qquad 
\widetilde H_r(\Delta)=H_r(\Delta)
\quad\text{for }r>0.
\]
For later use, we call $C_{-1}(\Delta)$ the \defn{augmentation part} and the non-negative degree part $C_\bullet(\Delta)$ the \defn{resolution part} of $\widetilde C_\bullet(\Delta)$.
If clarification of ground ring is needed, we will add `$;R$' inside the brackets, for example, $C_\bullet(\Delta;R)$ for the $R$-valued chain complex.

\subsection{More on order complexes}
\label{subsec:topological-tools}

A simplicial complex $K$ is a \defn{cone with vertex $p$} if $p\in V(K)$ and $\sigma\cup\{p\}\in K$ for every $\sigma\in K$. A cone has zero reduced homology over $R$.  To see this, order its vertices with $p$ first and define
\[
h_{-1}:R\longrightarrow C_0(K),\qquad 1\longmapsto\langle p\rangle,
\]
and, for $k\geq0$, define
\[
h_k:C_k(K)\longrightarrow C_{k+1}(K),\qquad
\langle v_0<\cdots<v_k\rangle\longmapsto
\begin{cases}
\langle p<v_0<\cdots<v_k\rangle,&p\notin\{v_0,\ldots,v_k\},\\
0,&p\in\{v_0,\ldots,v_k\}.
\end{cases}
\]
These maps satisfy $\partial_0h_{-1}=\id_R$ and $\partial_{k+1}h_k+h_{k-1}\partial_k=\id_{C_k(K)}$ for $k\geq0$, so they contract the augmented complex $\widetilde C_\bullet(K)$. In particular, the order complex of a non-empty poset with a least or greatest element is a cone.

\begin{dfn}\label{def:link,face poset,sd}
Let $K$ be a finite simplicial complex on a vertex set $V$.  For a face $F\in K$, define its \defn{link} of $F$ in $K$ by
\[
\operatorname{lk}_K F:=\{G\subseteq V\setminus F:F\cup G\in K\}.
\]
Define the \defn{face poset} and the \defn{non-empty-face poset} of $K$ as
\[
\mathcal F(K):=\{\text{faces of }K\}, \quad \mathcal{F}^\times(K):=\mathcal{F}(K)\setminus\{\emptyset\},
\]
respectively, ordered by inclusion.  The \defn{barycentric subdivision} $\operatorname{sd}K$ of $K$ is the order complex of the non-empty-face poset of $K$.
\end{dfn}

For a finite simplicial complex $K$, write $|K|$ for its geometric realization.  We identify simplicial homology with the singular homology of the realization.  Barycentric subdivision preserves the geometric realization, and hence preserves reduced homology over $R$.

For a finite set $E$ with $|E|\geq2$, the order complex of the power set $2^E$, whose partial order given by set inclusion, is a $(|E|-1)$-simplex on $E$. Its boundary is $\partial(2^E)=2^E\setminus\{E\}$, so
\begin{equation}
\Delta\bigl(2^E\setminus\{\emptyset,E\}\bigr)
=\Delta\bigl(\mathcal F^\times(\partial(2^E))\bigr)
=\operatorname{sd}(\partial(2^E)),\quad \text{and}\quad 
|\operatorname{sd}(\partial(2^E))|\cong S^{|E|-2}.
\label{eq:boolean-boundary-sphere}
\end{equation}
Thus omitting $E$ passes to the boundary of the simplex, and omitting $\emptyset$ passes to its non-empty-face poset.

\begin{lem}\label{lem:munkres}
Let $\mathcal F(K)_{>F}:=\{F'\in K\mid F\subsetneq F'\}$.  Then there is an isomorphism of simplicial complexes
\[
\Delta\bigl(\mathcal F(K)_{>F}\bigr)
\cong\operatorname{sd}(\operatorname{lk}_K F).
\]
In particular, we have $\widetilde{H}_k(\operatorname{lk}_KF)\cong \widetilde{H}_k(\Delta(\mathcal{F}(K)_{>F}))$ for all $k\ge -1$.
\end{lem}
\begin{proof}
The first part is known in \cite[Lemma 15.3]{Mun84}; we can also obtain it using the poset isomorphism
\newcommand{\mapsfrom}{\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\mapsto$}}}
\[
\mathcal F(K)_{>F}:=\{F'\in K\mid F\subsetneq F'\} \rightarrow
\mathcal{F}^\times(\operatorname{lk}_K F),
\qquad F'\mapsto F'\setminus F, \text{ with inverse } F\cup H \mapsfrom  H.
\]
The second part follows from
\[
\widetilde{H}_k(\operatorname{lk}_KF)\cong \widetilde{H}_k\big(\operatorname{sd}(\operatorname{lk}_KF)\big) \cong \widetilde{H}_k\big(\Delta(\mathcal{F}(K)_{>F})\big),
\]
where the first isomorphism uses the fact that barycentric subdivision preserves reduced homology \cite[Theorem 17.2]{Mun84}.
\end{proof}

We also recall an order homotopy theorem for maps between order complexes.

Let $f:Q\to Q'$ be an order-preserving map between two finite posets $Q,Q'$.  Define, for all $k\ge 0$, a map $f_{\#,k}:C_k(\Delta(Q))\to C_k(\Delta(Q'))$ given by
\[
\langle x_0<\cdots<x_k\rangle \mapsto
\begin{cases}
\langle f(x_0)<\cdots<f(x_k)\rangle, & f(x_0),\ldots,f(x_k)\text{ are pairwise distinct},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
We also set $f_{\#,-1}=\id_R$. Then $f_\#$ is a chain map on the chain complexes and their augmentations.

\begin{lem}\label{lem:order homotopy}
Let $f,g:Q\to Q'$ be order-preserving maps between finite posets, and suppose that $f(q)\leq g(q)$ for every $q\in Q$. The induced chain maps $f_\#,g_\#$ are chain homotopic.
\end{lem}
\begin{proof}
This is the chain complex version of \cite[Theorem 10.11]{Bjo95}; see also \cite[Theorem 13.3]{Mun84}. We can verify it directly as follows. For $k\geq0$, define a map $h_k:C_k(\Delta(Q))\longrightarrow C_{k+1}(\Delta(Q'))$ by
\[
\langle x_0<\cdots<x_k\rangle\mapsto
\sum_{j=0}^k(-1)^j
\langle f(x_0)\leq\cdots\leq f(x_j)\leq g(x_j)\leq\cdots\leq g(x_k)\rangle.
\]
Every chain displayed on the right is weakly increasing, so to get well-defined map we interpret each summand containing repeated members as zero.  Set also $h_{-1}=0$.  Then it is routine to check that
\[
\partial_{k+1}^{Q'} h_k+h_{k-1}\partial_k^{Q} = g_{\#,k}-f_{\#,k},
\]
which gives the desired homotopy.
\end{proof}


The following consequence of Alexander duality will be useful.


\begin{lem}\label{lem:split-boundary-subset-chains}
Assume that $|P|\geq3$, and let $K$ be a proper subcomplex of
$\Delta\bigl(2^P\setminus\{\emptyset,P\}\bigr)$. Then the boundary map
$\partial_{|P|-2}:C_{|P|-2}(K)\longrightarrow C_{|P|-3}(K)$ has an $R$-linear left inverse. 
\end{lem}
\begin{proof}
Put $d:=|P|-2\ge 1$. If $K=\emptyset$ there is nothing to prove, so assume $K\ne\emptyset$.
Since $P$ is finite, each $C_q(K)=C_q(K;\mathbb Z)\otimes_{\mathbb Z}R$ is a finitely generated free $R$-module. 
Let
\[
  C^q(K;R):=\Hom_R\bigl(C_q(K),R\bigr),\qquad
  \delta^q:=\Hom_R(\partial_{q+1},R)\colon C^q(K;R)\to C^{q+1}(K;R)
\]
be the cochain complex.
Each $C^q(K;R)\cong C^q(K;\mathbb Z)\otimes_{\mathbb Z}R$ is again finitely generated free over $R$.

We first show that $\delta^{d-1}\colon C^{d-1}(K;\mathbb Z)\to C^{d}(K;\mathbb Z)$ is surjective.
Indeed, since $|\Delta(2^P\setminus\{\emptyset,P\})|\cong S^{d}$ by \eqref{eq:boolean-boundary-sphere}, $K$ is a
proper subcomplex, $|K|$ is a compact, locally contractible, non-empty, proper subspace of $S^d$.
In particular, we have $S^d\setminus|K|\neq\emptyset$, and Alexander duality \cite[Cor.~3.45]{Hat02} gives
\[
  H^{d}(K;\mathbb Z)\;\cong\;\widetilde H^{d}(|K|;\mathbb Z)\;\cong\;\widetilde H_{-1}(S^d\setminus|K|;\mathbb Z)\;=\;0,
\]
where the first isomorphism uses $d\ge1$, and the last equality holds because reduced homology in degree $-1$ vanishes for a non-empty space.
As $K$ has no simplices of dimension strictly larger than $d$, we have $C^{d+1}(K;\mathbb Z)=0$, so $H^d(K;\mathbb Z)=\operatorname{coker}\delta^{d-1}$, which means that $\delta^{d-1}$ is surjective, as claimed.

Now we base change back to $R$.
Since $-\otimes_{\mathbb Z}R$ is right exact, $\delta^{d-1}\colon C^{d-1}(K;R)\to C^d(K;R)$ is still surjective with a free (hence, projective) codomain, so there is an $R$-linear right inverse $s: C^d(K;R)\to C^{d-1}(K;R)$ of $\delta^{d-1}$.

Now the claim follows by dualizing back to the chain groups.
Namely, since the chain groups are finitely generated free over $R$, we have $C_q(K;R)\cong \Hom_R(C^q(K;R),R)$ and $\Hom_R(\delta^{d-1},R)=\partial_{d}$.  Applying $\Hom_R(-,R)$ to $\delta^{d-1}\circ s=\mathrm{id}$ thus yields the required 
$\Hom_R(s,R)\circ\partial_d=\mathrm{id}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Finite posets and distributive lattices}

Recall that a \defn{lattice} is a poset $L$ in which every pair $x,y\in L$ has a greatest lower bound, called the \defn{meet} and denoted by $x\wedge y$, and a least upper bound, called the \defn{join} and denoted by $x\vee y$. A lattice is \defn{distributive} if
\[
x\wedge(y\vee z)
 =(x\wedge y)\vee(x\wedge z),
\qquad
x\vee(y\wedge z)
 =(x\vee y)\wedge(x\vee z)
\]
for all $x,y,z\in L$. A finite lattice has a least element $0$ and a greatest element $1$.

For a finite poset $P$, we denote by $\max(P)$ the set of its maximal elements. An \defn{order ideal} of $P$ is a subset $X\subseteq P$ such that if $y\in X$ and $x\leq y$ then $x\in X$. We denote by $\mathcal J(P)$ the set of order ideals of $P$, ordered by inclusion. It is a finite distributive lattice with $X\wedge Y=X\cap Y$ and $X\vee Y=X\cup Y$.

Conversely, Birkhoff's fundamental theorem of finite distributive lattices states that every finite distributive lattice is isomorphic to $\mathcal J(P)$ for some finite poset $P$; see, for example, \cite{Gra03}.

The \defn{Hasse graph} of a finite poset $Q$ is the undirected graph whose vertex set is $Q$, in which two elements are adjacent if one covers the other, i.e. an edge is given by $\{x,y\}$ with $x<y$ and there is no $z\in Q$ satisfying $x<z<y$. We write $d_Q(x,y)$, or simply $d(x,y)$, for the corresponding graph distance. Moreover, for subsets $X,Y\subseteq Q$, their \defn{symmetric difference} is $X\SDiff Y:=(X\setminus Y)\sqcup(Y\setminus X)$.

\begin{lem}\label{lem:symmetric-difference-identities}
Let $X,Y,Z$ be subsets of a finite set. Then the following hold.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\arabic*)},ref=\arabic*]
\item\label{itm:symdiff-associativity} $X\SDiff Y=Y\SDiff X$, $X\SDiff X=\emptyset$, and $(X\SDiff Y)\SDiff Z=X\SDiff(Y\SDiff Z)$.
\item\label{itm:symdiff-cancellation} $(X\SDiff Y)\SDiff(X\SDiff Z)=Y\SDiff Z$, $X\SDiff(X\SDiff Y)=Y.$
\item\label{itm:symdiff-intersection} Let $\nabla^{Y,Z}_X:=(X\cap Y)\cup (X\cap Z)\cup (Y\cap Z)$ (note that the position of $X,Y,Z$ around $\nabla$ does not matter).  Then $(X\SDiff Y)\cap (X\SDiff Z) = \bigl(X\setminus(Y\cup Z)\bigr)\sqcup\bigl((Y\cap Z)\setminus X\bigr) = X\SDiff \nabla^{Y,Z}_X$.
%\[
%\begin{aligned}
% (X\SDiff Y)\cap(X\SDiff Z)
% &=\bigl(X\setminus(Y\cup Z)\bigr)\sqcup\bigl((Y\cap Z)\setminus X\bigr)\\
%&=X\SDiff\bigl((X\cap Y)\cup(X\cap Z)\cup(Y\cap Z)\bigr).
%\end{aligned}
%\]

\item\label{itm:symdiff-difference} $ (X\SDiff Y)\setminus(X\SDiff Z) =\bigl(X\cap(Z\setminus Y)\bigr) \sqcup\bigl((Y\setminus Z)\setminus X\bigr).$

\item\label{itm:symdiff-inclusion} If $Y\subseteq Z$, then $ (X\SDiff Y)\subseteq(X\SDiff Z) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; X\cap(Z\setminus Y)=\emptyset$.
%\old{In such a case, we have $X\SDiff Z=(X\SDiff Y)\sqcup(Z\setminus Y)$. \comment{is this used?}} \old{no, we can delete it}
More generally, we have 
\[
(X\SDiff Y)\subseteq(X\SDiff Z)\quad \Longleftrightarrow\quad 
 \begin{cases}
     X\cap(Z\setminus Y)=\emptyset,\\
     Y\setminus Z\subseteq X.
 \end{cases}
\]

\item\label{itm:symdiff-interval} If $(X\SDiff Z)\subseteq(X\SDiff Y)$, then
\[
\begin{aligned}
 X\SDiff Y&=(X\SDiff Z)\sqcup(Z\setminus Y)\sqcup(Y\setminus Z),\\
 X\SDiff(Y\cap Z)&=(X\SDiff Z)\sqcup(Z\setminus Y),\\
 X\SDiff(Y\cup Z)&=(X\SDiff Z)\sqcup(Y\setminus Z).
\end{aligned}
\]

\item\label{itm:symdiff-cardinality} $|Y|=|X|+|X\SDiff Y|$ if and only if  $X\subseteq Y$.
\end{enumerate}

\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
(1), (2), (3) are elementary set theory exercise which we leave for the reader to check.

(4)\; Intersecting $(X\SDiff Y)\setminus(X\SDiff Z)$ with $X$ yields the left set in the decomposition, and removing $X$ yields the second.

(5)\; The `more generally' part follows from \textup{(\ref{itm:symdiff-difference})} as left-hand side is equivalent to $(X\SDiff Y)\setminus (X\SDiff Z)=\emptyset$.  The first part of the claim follows from this general case immediately.

(6)\; The hypothesis $(X\SDiff Z)\subseteq(X\SDiff Y)$ gives $Z\setminus Y\subseteq X$ and $(Y\setminus Z)\cap X=\emptyset$ by \textup{(\ref{itm:symdiff-inclusion})} (with $Y$ and $Z$ exchanged). Thus removing $Z\setminus Y$ from $Z$, or adjoining $Y\setminus Z$ to $Z$, adds precisely the corresponding set to $X\SDiff Z$. The three identities then follow from
\[
\begin{aligned}
 Y&=\bigl(Z\setminus(Z\setminus Y)\bigr)\sqcup(Y\setminus Z),\\
 Y\cap Z&=Z\setminus(Z\setminus Y),\\
 Y\cup Z&=Z\sqcup(Y\setminus Z).
\end{aligned}
\]
Note that the unions are disjoint because $Z\setminus Y$ and $Y\setminus Z$ are disjoint from $X\SDiff Z$ and from each other.

(7)\; We always have $|X|+|X\SDiff Y|-|Y|=2|X\setminus Y|$, and so $|Y|=|X|+|X\SDiff Y|$ holds precisely when $X\setminus Y=\emptyset$, or equivalently, $X\subseteq Y$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lem}\label{lem:d(X,Y)}
For any $X,Y,Z\in\mathcal J(P)$, one has
\[
d(X,Y)=|X\SDiff Y|, \quad\text{ and }\quad 
d(X,Y)=d(X,Z)+d(Z,Y)
\Longleftrightarrow
X\SDiff Z\subseteq X\SDiff Y.
\]
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
Every edge in the Hasse graph of $\mathcal J(P)$ changes an order ideal by exactly one element. Hence every path from $X$ to $Y$ has length at least $|X\SDiff Y|$. Conversely, one may first remove the elements of $X\setminus Y$, choosing a maximal remaining element at each step, and then add the elements of $Y\setminus X$, choosing a minimal missing element at each step. Every intermediate subset is an order ideal, and this gives a path of length $|X\SDiff Y|$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:symmetric-difference-identities}\textup{(\ref{itm:symdiff-cancellation})}, $Z\SDiff Y=(X\SDiff Z)\SDiff(X\SDiff Y)$. Hence the second assertion follows from the distance formula and Lemma~\ref{lem:symmetric-difference-identities}\textup{(\ref{itm:symdiff-cardinality})}, applied to $X\SDiff Z$ and $X\SDiff Y$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lem}[{cf.\ \cite[Lemma~6.3 and Corollary~7.24(2)]{CX26}}]
\label{lem:projective-relative-injective}
For $X\in\mathcal J(P)$, the projective $\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}$-module $P_X$ is relative injective if and only if $P\setminus X\in\mathcal J(P)$. In this case, $P_X\cong I_{P\setminus X}$ and $P_{P\setminus X}\cong I_X$.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
$\underline{\Rightarrow}$: Suppose that $P_X$ is relative injective, that is, $P_X\in\add D(\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)})$. Choose a maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$ of $R$. Since $\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}$ is free over $R$ of finite rank, we have
\[
D(\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)})\otimes_R R/\mathfrak m
\cong
\Hom_{R/\mathfrak m}
\bigl(\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}\otimes_R R/\mathfrak m,
R/\mathfrak m\bigr).
\]
Thus $P_X\otimes_R R/\mathfrak m$ is injective over $\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}\otimes_R R/\mathfrak m$. By \cite[Lemma~6.3 and Corollary~7.24(2)]{CX26}, we obtain $P\setminus X\in\mathcal J(P)$.

$\underline{\Leftarrow}:$ Suppose that $P\setminus X\in\mathcal J(P)$. By Lemma \ref{lem:d(X,Y)}, for every $Y\in\mathcal J(P)$, we have $d(X,Y)+d(Y,P\setminus X)=|P|=d(X,P\setminus X)$, which then implies that $b_{XY}b_{Z,\,P\setminus X}=\delta_{Y,Z}b_{X,\,P\setminus X}$ for any $Y,Z\in\mathcal J(P)$. Define
\[
\phi:P_X\longrightarrow I_{P\setminus X},
\qquad
\phi(p)(q):=
\text{the coefficient of $b_{X,\,P\setminus X}$ in $pq$},
\]
where $p\in P_X=e_X\Sigma_{\mathcal{J}(P)}$ and $q\in\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}e_{P\setminus X}$. It is routine to verify that this is a homomorphism of modules. The multiplication  $b_{XY}b_{Z,\,P\setminus X}=\delta_{Y,Z}b_{X,\,P\setminus X}$ implies that $\phi$ sends the basis $\{b_{XY}\}_{Y\in\mathcal J(P)}$ to the dual $R$-basis of $\{b_{Y,\,P\setminus X}\}_{Y\in\mathcal J(P)}$. Thus $P_X\cong I_{P\setminus X}$ and is relative injective. Swapping $X$ and $P\setminus X$ gives $P_{P\setminus X}\cong I_X$.
\end{proof}

\begin{eg}\label{eg:posets-at-most-two-elements}
Up to isomorphism, there are exactly four posets $P$ with $|P|\leq2$. In the following, $A_n$ denotes the path graph on $n$ vertices.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $P=\emptyset$, then $\mathcal J(P)=\{\emptyset\}$ and $\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}\cong R$.  Thus, $\Sigma_P$ is self-injective ($\idim^{\lat} \Sigma_P=0$).

\item If $P=\{\ast\}$, then $\mathcal J(P)=\{\emptyset \subset (\ast)\}$ and $\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}\cong\Sigma_{A_2}$. The algebra $\Sigma_{A_2}$ is given by the quiver
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large]
{1} & {2}
\arrow["{b_{12}}", from=1-1, to=1-2, bend left]
\arrow["{b_{21}}", from=1-2, to=1-1, bend left]
\end{tikzcd} \;\; \text{with relations } b_{12}b_{21}=0=b_{21}b_{12}.
\]
Then by Lemma~\ref{lem:projective-relative-injective} $\Sigma_{A_2}$ is self-injective.

\item If $P=\{a,b\}$ with $a,b$ incomparable, then $\mathcal J(P)$ is the Boolean lattice of rank two, which is the direct product of two copies of $\{1<2\}$.  The property $P\setminus X\in\mathcal J(P)$ for every $X\in\mathcal J(P)$ still holds, and so it follows from Lemma \ref{lem:projective-relative-injective} that $\Sigma_{J(P)}$ is self-injective.
Note that the Hasse graph of $J(P)$ is the square $Q_2$, which is the Cartesian product of two copies of $A_2$, and so it follows from \cite[Proposition~3.2]{CX26} that
\[
\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}
\cong\Sigma_{Q_2}
\cong\Sigma_{A_2}\otimes_R\Sigma_{A_2},
\]
and one can then deduce self-injective of $\Sigma_{\mathcal{J}(P)}$ by the fact that tensor product over the base ring $R$ preserves self-injectivity.

\item If $P=\{a<b\}$, then $\mathcal J(P)=\{\emptyset \subset (a)\subset (a<b)\}\cong \{1<2<3\}$ and $\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}\cong\Sigma_{A_3}$. The algebra $\Sigma_{A_3}$ is given by the quiver
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large]
{1} & {2} & {3}
\arrow["{b_{12}}", from=1-1, to=1-2, bend left]
\arrow["{b_{21}}", from=1-2, to=1-1, bend left]
\arrow["{b_{23}}", from=1-2, to=1-3, bend left]
\arrow["{b_{32}}", from=1-3, to=1-2, bend left]
\end{tikzcd}
\]
with every path of length two that returns to its starting vertex equal to zero.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:projective-relative-injective}, $P_1\cong I_3$ and $P_3\cong I_1$, whereas $P_2$ is not relative injective. Let $\{f_i\}_{i=1}^3$ be the $R$-basis of $I_2=D(\Sigma_{A_3}e_2)$ dual to $\{b_{i2}\}_{i=1}^3$. There is an exact sequence
\[
0\longrightarrow P_2
\xrightarrow{\ \iota\ }P_1\oplus P_3
\xrightarrow{\ \pi\ }I_2\longrightarrow0,
\]
where $\iota(p)=(b_{12}p,-b_{32}p)$ and $\pi(p,q)=f_1p+f_3q$. This sequence is a relative injective coresolution of $P_2$ of length one, and $\idim^{\lat}\Sigma_{A_3}=1$. The same equality holds on the left by Corollaries~\ref{cor:relative-ordinary-injective-dimension} and~\ref{cor:distance-gorenstein-criterion}.
For the special case where $R$ is a field, see \cite[Proposition~7.6]{CX26}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{eg}

\subsection{Order complexes of strict upsets}
\label{subsec:strict-upsets}

Let $P$ be a finite poset, fix $X\in\mathcal J(P)$.

We write $\underline{Z}:=X\SDiff Z$ (and $\underline{Z}_X$ if clarification is needed). Consider the poset
\[\mathcal D_X := \{X\SDiff Z \mid Z\in\mathcal J(P)\},\] 
ordered by inclusion.

For $Z\in\mathcal J(P)$, let $\D_X^{>\underline{Z}}$ be the upset of $\D_X$ containing all elements strictly larger than $Z$; we call this \defn{strict upset above $\underline{Z}$} for convenience. Denote by
\[
\Delta_X:=\Delta(\D_X), \qquad \text{and} \qquad \Delta^{>\underline{Z}}:=\Delta_X^{>\underline{Z}}:=\Delta(\D_X^{>\underline{Z}})
\]
the associated order complexes.  Note that the vertices of $\Delta^{>\underline{Z}}$ are the elements $\underline{T}>\underline{Z}$, and its $r$-dimensional faces are the strict chains $\underline{T_0}<\underline{T_1}<\cdots<\underline{T_r}$, which means that it is a subcomplex of $\Delta_X$.

Consequently,
\[
 \widetilde H_r(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}}_{[1]})=0
 \quad\text{except for}\quad
 \widetilde H_0(\Delta^{>\underline{3}}_{[1]})\cong R(\langle \underline{13}\rangle -  \langle\underline{23}\rangle),
 \quad
 \widetilde H_{-1}(\Delta^{>\underline{13}}_{[1]})\cong R,
 \quad

\]
\end{eg}

\section{A projective resolution from strict chains}
\label{sec:candidate-resolution}

We retain the notation and assumptions of Subsection~\ref{subsec:strict-upsets}. In this section we construct a complex $\mathcal P_\bullet$ of projective $A$-modules indexed by the strict chains in $\D_X$, equip it with an augmentation to $I_X$, and prove that the augmented complex is exact.

\subsection{The candidate complex}

To distinguish between different copies of the same module in a direct sum, we will use $P_Z^{(i)}$ to denote the copy of $P_Z$ labeled by $i$ in some indexing set.  Similarly, for $p\in P_Z$, we write $p^{(i)}$ for the element $p$ in the $i$-labelled copy of $P_Z$; this is nothing but a compact way to write $\iota_i(p)$ where $\iota_i$ is the canonical inclusion of $P_Z^{(i)}$ into the direct sum that contains it.

For $k\geq0$, we write
\[
 \underline{Z}_\ast=(\underline{Z}_0<\cdots<\underline{Z}_k)
 \in\Delta(\mathcal D_X)_k
\]
for a strict chain and  it is understood that $Z_i$ is the order ideal such that $Z_i \SDiff X = \underline{Z}_i$. For $0\leq i\leq k$, define
\[
 \underline{Z}_\ast^{\widehat{i}}
 :=(\underline{Z}_0<\cdots<\widehat{\underline{Z}_i}<\cdots<\underline{Z}_k)
\]
to be the strict chain obtained by deleting $\underline{Z}_i$. When $k=0$, the chain $\underline{Z}_\ast^{\widehat{0}}$ is empty.

For $k\geq0$, define $\mathcal{P}_k$ to be the direct sum of projective $A$-module $P_Z$ over all $k$-chains in $\Delta_{X,k}$. Observe that, when $k=0$ (so $\Delta_{X,0}=\mathcal{D}_X=\mathcal{J}(P)$), we have
\[
\mathcal{P}_0 = \bigoplus_{Z\in\mathcal{J}(P)} P_Z^{(\underline{Z})} \cong \bigoplus_{Z\in\mathcal{J}(P)} P_Z\otimes_{R} C_{-1}(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}})\]
via $p^{(\underline{Z})}\mapsto p\otimes 1\in P_Z\otimes_{R}C_{-1}(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}})$. On the other hand, when $k>0$, a chain $\underline{Z}_\ast=(\underline{Z}_0<\underline{Z}_1<\cdots<\underline{Z}_k) \in \Delta_{X,k}$ can be identified with the $(k-1)$-simplex $\underline{Z}_\ast^{\widehat{0}}$ of $\Delta^{>\underline{Z}}$. This yields an isomorphism
\begin{equation}
\mathcal P_k := \bigoplus_{\underline{Z}_\ast\in \Delta_{X,k}} P_{Z_0}^{(\underline{Z}_\ast)} \cong
\bigoplus_{Z\in\mathcal J(P)}
P_Z\otimes_{R}C_{k-1}(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}}), \qquad  p^{(\underline{Z}_\ast)} \mapsto \begin{cases}
    p\otimes \langle \underline{Z}_\ast^{\widehat{0}} \rangle, & k>0;\\ p\otimes 1, & k=0.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:candidate-terms}
\end{equation}

Note that, for $Z,T\in\mathcal J(P)$ with $\underline{Z}<\underline{T}$ (equivalently, $d(X,T)=d(X,Z)+d(Z,T)$), we have a right $A$-module homomorphism
$$\mu_{T,Z}\colon P_Z\longrightarrow P_T, \qquad p\longmapsto b_{TZ}p,$$ given by by left-multiplying $b_{TZ}\in e_TAe_Z$.
For $k>0$, define an $A$-module homomorphism $d_k\colon\mathcal P_k\longrightarrow\mathcal P_{k-1}$ by
\begin{equation}
 d_k\bigl(p^{(\underline{Z}_\ast)}\bigr)
:=
\bigl(\mu_{Z_1,Z_0}(p)\bigr)^{(\underline{Z}_\ast^{\widehat{0}})}
+ \sum_{i=1}^k
(-1)^i  p^{(\underline{Z}_\ast^{\widehat{i}})}.
 \label{eq:candidate-differential}
\end{equation}

\begin{prop}\label{prop:resolution-differential}
The maps $d_k$ satisfy $d_{k-1}d_k=0$ for every $k\geq2$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Fix $\underline{Z}_\ast=(\underline{Z}_0<\cdots<\underline{Z}_k)$. For $0\leq i\leq k$, write \[
\left.d_k\right|_{P_{Z_0}^{(\underline{Z}_\ast)}}
= \sum_{i=0}^k(-1)^i\varepsilon_i^{\underline{Z}_\ast},
\]
where the homomorphisms $\varepsilon_i^{\underline{Z}_\ast}$ are
\begin{align}\label{eq:face map}
\varepsilon_i^{\underline{Z}_\ast}\colon
P_{Z_0}^{(\underline{Z}_\ast)}
\longrightarrow
\mathcal P_{k-1}, \quad \varepsilon_i^{\underline{Z}_\ast}
\bigl(p^{(\underline{Z}_\ast)}\bigr)
:=
\begin{cases}
\bigl(\mu_{Z_1,Z_0}(p)\bigr)^{
 (\underline{Z}_\ast^{\widehat{0}})},
 & i=0,\\[1mm]
p^{(\underline{Z}_\ast^{\widehat{i}})},
 & i>0.
\end{cases}
\end{align}

We claim that
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_i^{\underline{Z}_\ast^{\widehat{j}}}
\varepsilon_j^{\underline{Z}_\ast}
=
\varepsilon_{j-1}^{\underline{Z}_\ast^{\widehat{i}}}
\varepsilon_i^{\underline{Z}_\ast}
\qquad
(0\leq i<j\leq k).
\label{eq:face-relation}
\end{equation}
Indeed, if $i>0$, both sides are the identity map between the same two labelled copies of $P_{Z_0}$. On the other hand, if $i=0$, then the case when and $j>1$ implies that both sides are induced by $\mu_{Z_1,Z_0}$. It remains to consider $i=0$ and $j=1$. In this case, the two sides are induced respectively by $\mu_{Z_2,Z_0}$ and $\mu_{Z_2,Z_1}\mu_{Z_1,Z_0}$. Since $\underline{Z}_0<\underline{Z}_1<\underline{Z}_2$, the corresponding distances are additive, and hence $b_{Z_2Z_1}b_{Z_1Z_0}=b_{Z_2Z_0}$. Therefore $\mu_{Z_2,Z_1}\mu_{Z_1,Z_0}=\mu_{Z_2,Z_0}$, which proves \eqref{eq:face-relation}.

For every pair $0\leq i<j\leq k$, the two ways of deleting the $i$-th and $j$-th members of $\underline{Z}_\ast$ occur in $d_{k-1}d_k$ with respective coefficients $(-1)^j(-1)^i=(-1)^{i+j}$ and $(-1)^i(-1)^{j-1}=-(-1)^{i+j}$. By \eqref{eq:face-relation}, the corresponding homomorphisms are equal, so these two terms cancel.  All terms in $d_{k-1}d_k$ arise uniquely in this way, and therefore $d_{k-1}d_k=0$.
\end{proof}

For each $Y\in\mathcal J(P)$, we have a free $R$-module $I_Xe_Y=D(e_YAe_X)$ of rank $1$.  Let $\phi_Y\colon P_Y\to I_X$ be the homomorphism determined by sending $e_Y$ to the canonical $R$-basis element dual to $b_{YX}\in e_YAe_X$. If $\underline{Z}_0<\underline{Z}_1$, then the corresponding distances are additive and $b_{Z_1Z_0}b_{Z_0X}=b_{Z_1X}$.  Consequently, $\phi_{Z_1}\mu_{Z_1,Z_0}=\phi_{Z_0}$ whenever $\underline{Z}_0<\underline{Z}_1$. Define
\[
 d_0:=\bigl(\phi_Y\bigr)_{Y\in\mathcal J(P)}\colon\mathcal P_0=\bigoplus_{Y\in\mathcal J(P)}P_Y\longrightarrow I_X.
\]
For \(\underline{Z}_0<\underline{Z}_1\), one has $d_0d_1\bigl(p^{(\underline{Z}_0<\underline{Z}_1)}\bigr)=\phi_{Z_1}(b_{Z_1Z_0}p)-\phi_{Z_0}(p)=0$. Hence, by Proposition \ref{prop:resolution-differential}, we obtain a chain complex
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_\bullet: \quad \cdots\longrightarrow\mathcal P_2\xrightarrow{d_2}\mathcal P_1\xrightarrow{d_1}\mathcal P_0\xrightarrow{d_0}I_X\longrightarrow0.
 \label{eq:candidate-complex}
\end{equation}
We call $\widetilde{\mathcal P}_\bullet$ the \defn{augmented candidate complex} and $\mathcal{P}_\bullet$ is its resolution part.

The main theorem we want to show is the following.
\begin{thm}\label{thm:exactness}
The complex $\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_\bullet$ is exact, i.e. $\mathcal{P}_\bullet$ defines a projective resolution of $I_X$.
\end{thm}

Note that the projective resolution constructed above gives an upper bound on $\pdim_A I_X$ through the lengths of strict chains in $\D_X$. Together with Corollary~\ref{cor:distance-gorenstein-criterion}, this yields the Iwanaga--Gorenstein property of $A$.

Counting the lengths of strict chains in $\mathcal D_X$ gives the following consequence of Theorem~\ref{thm:exactness}.

\begin{cor}\label{cor:distributive-ig}
Assume that $P\neq\emptyset$. Then, for every $X\in\mathcal J(P)$,
\[
\pdim_{\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}}I_X\leq |P|-1.
\]
In particular, if $R$ is Gorenstein, then $\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}$ is a Gorenstein $R$-algebra. If $R$ is Iwanaga--Gorenstein, then $\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}$ is Iwanaga--Gorenstein and
\[
\idim(\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)})_{\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}}
=\idim{}_{\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}}\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}
\leq |P|-1+\idim_R R.
\]
\end{cor}

\begin{proof}
Fix $X\in\mathcal J(P)$. If $P\setminus X\in\mathcal J(P)$, then $I_X\cong P_{P\setminus X}$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:projective-relative-injective}, so $I_X$ is projective. Otherwise $P\notin\mathcal D_X$, so every strict chain in $\mathcal D_X$ has at most $|P|$ members and $\mathcal P_k=0$ for $k\geq|P|$. Theorem~\ref{thm:exactness} therefore gives $\pdim_{\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}}I_X\leq|P|-1$. The remaining assertions follow from Corollary~\ref{cor:distance-gorenstein-criterion}.
\end{proof}

\begin{rmk}
If $L\cong\mathcal J(P)$, then $P$ is isomorphic to the poset of join-irreducible elements of $L$. Thus the bounds can also be stated in terms of the number of join-irreducible elements of $L$.
\end{rmk}

\subsection{Exactness of the candidate complex}
We prove exactness after right multiplication by each vertex idempotent $e_W$.  The first lemma records the multiplication criterion used in both steps of the argument.

\begin{lem}\label{lem:multiplication-criterion}
Let $U,V,W\in\mathcal J(P)$ with $\underline{U}\leq \underline{V}$.  Then $b_{VU}b_{UW}\neq0 \; \Leftrightarrow\; (\underline{V}\setminus \underline{U})\cap \underline{W}=\emptyset$.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}We have
\[
 b_{VU}b_{UW}\neq0
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad W\,\SDiff\, U\subseteq W\,\SDiff\, V
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \underline{W}\,\SDiff\,\underline{Z}\subseteq\underline{W}\,\SDiff\,\underline{Y},
\]
where the first equivalence is Lemma~\ref{lem:d(X,Y)}, the second follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:symmetric-difference-identities}\textup{(\ref{itm:symdiff-cancellation})}. The claim then follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:symmetric-difference-identities}\textup{(\ref{itm:symdiff-difference})} by taking $(X,Y,Z)=(W,V,U)$.
\end{proof}

Fix $W\in\mathcal J(P)$.  Right multiplication by $e_W$ gives the complex
\begin{equation*}
 \widetilde{\mathcal P}_\bullet e_W:\quad
 \cdots\longrightarrow\mathcal P_1e_W
 \longrightarrow\mathcal P_0e_W
 \longrightarrow I_Xe_W\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation*}
We first replace it by a smaller homotopy equivalent subcomplex.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:lower-deformation-retract}
For $k\geq0$, put
\[
 \mathcal P_k^{\leq W}
 :=\bigoplus_{\substack{
 \underline{Z}_\ast=(\underline{Z}_0<\cdots<\underline{Z}_k)\in\Delta_{X,k}\\
 \underline{Z}_k\leq \underline{W}}}
 (P_{Z_0}e_W)^{(\underline{Z}_\ast)}.
\]
Together with $I_Xe_W$ in degree $-1$, these $R$-modules form a subcomplex $\widetilde{\mathcal P}_\bullet^{\leq W}$ of $\widetilde{\mathcal P}_\bullet e_W$.  Its inclusion
\[
 \iota_W\colon
 \widetilde{\mathcal P}_\bullet^{\leq W}
 \longrightarrow
 \widetilde{\mathcal P}_\bullet e_W
\]
is a homotopy equivalence.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Since $P_{Z_0}e_W=R\, b_{Z_0W}$, the differential in \eqref{eq:candidate-differential} evaluates to
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 d_k\bigl((b_{Z_0W})^{(\underline{Z}_\ast)}\bigr)
 &=\bigl(b_{Z_1Z_0}b_{Z_0W}\bigr)^{(\underline{Z}_\ast^{\widehat{0}})}
 +\sum_{i=1}^k(-1)^i
 (b_{Z_0W})^{(\underline{Z}_\ast^{\widehat{i}})}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:evaluated-differential}
\end{equation}
Deleting a member of a chain whose largest member is at most $\underline{W}$ preserves this property, so the displayed $R$-modules form a subcomplex.

For $\underline{Z}\in\D_X$, set $r_W(\underline{Z}):=\underline{Z}\cap \underline{W}$. This again lies in $\D_X$: indeed, recall from Lemma~\ref{lem:symmetric-difference-identities}\textup{(\ref{itm:symdiff-intersection})} that $\nabla^{Z,W}_X:=(X\cap Z)\cup(X\cap W)\cup(Z\cap W)$, which is also an order ideal, satisfies $\underline{Z}\cap\underline{W}=\underline{\nabla_X^{Z,W}}$.  Thus $r_W$ is an order-preserving map from $\D_X$ to $\D_{X}^{\leq W}:=\{\underline{Z}\in \mathcal{J}(P)\mid \underline{Z}\le \underline{W}\}$.

Fix $\underline{Z}_\ast=(\underline{Z}_0<\cdots<\underline{Z}_k)$ and let $T_i:=\nabla_X^{Z_i,W}$, so that $\underline{T_i}=\underline{Z}_i\cap \underline{W}$.  Define $\rho_{W,-1}:=\id_{I_Xe_W}$ and, for $k\geq0$, define
\[
 \rho_{W,k}\bigl((b_{Z_0W})^{(\underline{Z}_\ast)}\bigr)
 :=
 \begin{cases}
 (b_{T_0W})^{(\underline{T_0}<\cdots<\underline{T_k})},
     &\underline{T_0}<\cdots<\underline{T_k},\\
 0,&\underline{T_i}=\underline{T_{i+1}}\text{ for some }i.
 \end{cases}
\]
Then $\rho_W\iota_W=\id$ as graded maps.

It remains to construct the homotopy.  Put $h_{W,-1}:=0$ and, for $k\geq0$, define
\begin{equation*}
 \begin{aligned}
 h_{W,k}\bigl((b_{Z_0W})^{(\underline{Z}_\ast)}\bigr)
 :=\sum_{j=0}^k(-1)^j
 (b_{T_0W})^{(
 \underline{T_0}\leq\cdots\leq \underline{T_j}\leq
 \underline{Z}_j<\cdots<\underline{Z}_k)}.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
A summand is understood to be zero if two adjacent members of its indexing sequence are equal.  We now suppress the subscript $W$ from $\iota$, $\rho$, and $h$.

For $k=0$, a direct calculation gives $d_1h_0 =\id_{\mathcal P_0e_W}-\iota_0\rho_0$. For $k\geq1$, put
\[
 \tau_j
 :=(\underline{T_0}\leq\cdots\leq \underline{T_j}\leq
 \underline{Z}_j<\cdots<\underline{Z}_k).
\]
Expanding the differential gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 d_{k+1}h_k\bigl((b_{Z_0W})^{(\underline{Z}_\ast)}\bigr)
 &=(b_{Z_0T_0}b_{T_0W})^{(\underline{Z}_\ast)}\\
 &\quad+\sum_{j=1}^k(-1)^j
 (b_{T_1T_0}b_{T_0W})^{(\tau_j^{\widehat0})}\\
 &\quad+\sum_{j=0}^k\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}(-1)^{i+j}
 (b_{T_0W})^{(\tau_j^{\widehat i})}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Because $(\underline{U}\setminus r_W(\underline{U}))\cap \underline{W}=\emptyset$, Lemma~\ref{lem:multiplication-criterion} gives $b_{Z_iT_i}b_{T_iW}=b_{Z_iW}$.  The first term is therefore the original basis element, the single sum is $-h_{k-1}\bigl( (b_{Z_1Z_0}b_{Z_0W})^{(\underline{Z}_\ast^{\widehat{0}})} \bigr)$, and the double sum is
\[
 -\sum_{\ell=1}^k(-1)^\ell
 h_{k-1}\bigl((b_{Z_0W})^{(\underline{Z}_\ast^{\widehat{\ell}})}\bigr)
 -\iota_k\rho_k\bigl((b_{Z_0W})^{(\underline{Z}_\ast)}\bigr).
\]
Comparing this with the expansion of $h_{k-1}d_k$ yields
\begin{equation*}
 d_{k+1}h_k+h_{k-1}d_k
 =\id_{\mathcal P_ke_W}-\iota_k\rho_k
 \qquad(k\geq0).
\end{equation*}
For $k\geq1$, applying $d_k$ to this identity and comparing with the identity in degree $k-1$ gives
\[
 d_k(\id-\iota_k\rho_k)
 =(\id-\iota_{k-1}\rho_{k-1})d_k.
\]
Since $\iota_W$ is an injective chain map, it follows that $d_k\rho_k=\rho_{k-1}d_k$.  The same conclusion in degree zero follows by applying $d_0$ to the degree-zero homotopy identity.  Thus $\rho_W$ is a chain map and the displayed identity is the required homotopy.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Refining projective dimension}
\label{subsec:sharper-dimension-bound}


The cases $|P|\leq2$ are described in Example~\ref{eg:posets-at-most-two-elements}. For $|P|\geq3$, we will improve the bound in Corollary~\ref{cor:distributive-ig} by splitting the last differential of $\mathcal P_\bullet$. We first record the highest possible projective term, then use the topology of the boundary of a simplex to split its differential.

\begin{lem}\label{lem:chain-degree-bound}
Let $X\in\mathcal J(P)$ and suppose that $P\setminus X\notin\mathcal J(P)$. Then
\[
\Delta^{>\underline X}_r=\emptyset
\quad\text{for }r\geq|P|-1,
\qquad
\mathcal P_k=0
\quad\text{for }k\geq|P|.
\]
Moreover, the identification in \eqref{eq:candidate-terms} restricts to an isomorphism
\[
\mathcal P_{|P|-1}
\cong P_X\otimes_R C_{|P|-2}(\Delta^{>\underline X}).
\]
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
Since $P\setminus X\notin\mathcal J(P)$, we have $P\notin\mathcal D_X$. Every strict chain of non-empty members of $\mathcal D_X$ therefore has at most $|P|-1$ members, i.e. $\Delta^{>\underline X}_{|P|-1}=\emptyset$.   This also implies that every strict chain in $\mathcal D_X$ has at most $|P|$ members, so $\mathcal P_k=0$ for $k\geq|P|$. For the final isomorphism, note that every chain in $\mathcal D_X$ with $|P|$ members begins at $\emptyset=\underline X$, so the claim follows from \eqref{eq:candidate-terms}.
\end{proof}

Continue with $P=\{1<2\}\sqcup\{3\}$ and $X=\{1\}$ from Example~\ref{subsec:eg1}. The contribution coming from the complex $\Delta^{>\underline X}$ is the yellow-highlighted part in Figure \ref{fig:complex-picture}.  We have $C_1(\Delta^{>\underline X})\cong R^{\oplus 4}$, and so $\mathcal{P}_2\cong P_X^{\oplus 4}$ and $\mathcal{P}_k=0$ for $k\ge 3$.
\end{eg}


\begin{prop}\label{prop:n-2-inequalities}
Assume that $|P|\geq3$. Then, for every $X\in\mathcal J(P)$,
\[
\pdim_{\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}}I_X\leq |P|-2.
\]
If $R$ is Iwanaga--Gorenstein, then $\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}$ is Iwanaga--Gorenstein and
\[
\idim(\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)})_{\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}}
=\idim{}_{\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}}\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}
\leq |P|-2+\idim_R R.
\]
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Fix $X\in\mathcal J(P)$. If $P\setminus X\in\mathcal J(P)$, then $I_X$ is projective by Lemma~\ref{lem:projective-relative-injective}, and the projective dimension bound follows. Assume that $P\setminus X\notin\mathcal J(P)$.

\smallskip
\noindent
We first claim that there is an $R$-linear map
\[
\rho:
C_{|P|-3}(\Delta^{>\underline X})
\longrightarrow C_{|P|-2}(\Delta^{>\underline X})
\]
such that $\rho\partial_{|P|-2}=\id$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:split-boundary-subset-chains}, it suffices to show that $\Delta^{>\underline X}$ is a proper subcomplex of $\Delta(2^P\setminus\{\emptyset,P\})$.  Indeed, since $P\setminus X\notin\mathcal J(P)$, we have $P\notin\mathcal D_X$. 
By the assumption $P\setminus X\notin \mathcal J(P)$ and that $\underline{X}=\emptyset$, every vertex of $\Delta^{>\underline X}$ is a non-empty proper subset of $P$. 
Another consequence of $P\setminus X\notin\mathcal J(P)$ is that there exist $a<b$ in $P$ with $a\in X$ and $b\in P\setminus X$. 
Hence for any $T\in\mathcal J(P)$, the set $\underline{T}=X\SDiff T$ cannot contain both $a$ and $b$. 
In particular,  $\{a,b\}\notin\mathcal D_X$.  Since $|P|\geq3$, the set $\{a,b\}$ is a vertex of $\Delta(2^P\setminus\{\emptyset,P\})$. Thus $\Delta^{>\underline X}$ is a proper subcomplex of $\Delta(2^P\setminus\{\emptyset,P\})$, as required.

Now, we will construct the left inverse of $d_{|P|-1}:\mathcal{P}_{|P|-1}\to \mathcal{P}_{|P|-2}$. 
%By Lemma~\ref{lem:chain-degree-bound}, we have $\mathcal P_k=0$ for $k\geq|P|$ and $$
%\mathcal P_{|P|-1}
%\cong P_X\otimes_R C_{|P|-2}(\Delta^{>\underline X}).
%\]
Consider the projection of $\mathcal{P}_{|P|-2}$ onto the component $P_X\otimes_R C_{|P|-3}(\Delta^{> \underline{Z}})$ via  \eqref{eq:candidate-terms}, that is,
\[
\pi:\mathcal P_{|P|-2}
\longrightarrow P_X\otimes_R C_{|P|-3}(\Delta^{>\underline X}), \quad p^{(\underline Z_\ast)}\mapsto \begin{cases}
p\otimes\langle\underline Z_*^{\widehat{0}}\rangle,
& Z_0=X \text{ and } p\in P_{Z_0},\\
0,& \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

Consider a chain $\underline{Z}_\ast=(\underline Z_0<\underline Z_1<\cdots<\underline Z_{|P|-1})\in \D_X$ with $Z_0=X$ and let $p\in P_X$. 
Then by definition of the differential \eqref{eq:candidate-differential} we have
\begin{align}
\pi d_{|P|-1}
\bigl(p^{(\underline{Z}_\ast)}\bigr)
&=
\sum_{i=1}^{|P|-1}(-1)^i p\otimes
\langle\underline Z_1<\cdots<\widehat{\underline Z_i}
<\cdots<\underline Z_{|P|-1}\rangle\notag
\\
&=-p\otimes\partial_{|P|-2}
\langle\underline Z_1<\cdots<\underline Z_{|P|-1}\rangle. \label{eq: pi dP-1}
\end{align}
Combining with the isomorphism in Lemma \ref{lem:chain-degree-bound} we obtain the following composition
\[ \mathcal P_{|P|-1} \xrightarrow{d_{|P|-1}} \mathcal P_{|P|-2}
\xrightarrow{\pi}
P_X\otimes_R C_{|P|-3}(\Delta^{>\underline X})\xrightarrow{\ -\id_{P_X}\otimes\rho\ }
P_X\otimes_R C_{|P|-2}(\Delta^{>\underline X})\cong \mathcal P_{|P|-1}.
\]
Since $\rho$ is the left inverse of $\partial_{|P|-2}$, we get from \eqref{eq: pi dP-1} that
\[
(-\id_{P_X}\otimes\rho)\pi d_{|P|-1} = 
(-\id_{P_X}\otimes\rho) (-\id_{P_X}\otimes\partial_{|P|-2}) = \id_{P_X}\otimes \id_{C_{|P|-2}(\Delta^{>\underline X})},
\]
which means that $d_{|P|-1}$ is a split monomorphism. Thus we can truncate the resolution by one more term and get that
$\pdim_{\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}}I_X\leq|P|-2$.
The Iwanaga--Gorenstein assertion and the self-injective dimension bound now follow from Corollary~\ref{cor:distance-gorenstein-criterion}.
\end{proof}

\section{Morse reduction and the minimal projective resolution}
\label{sec:minimal-morse}

While we now know that ${\mathcal P}_\bullet$ is a projective resolution of $I_X$, it is far from being minimal.  To obtain a minimal resolution, we apply algebraic Morse theory \cite{CLZ26} to remove its invertible differential components. Throughout, we require the ground ring $R=\Bbbk$ to be a field.

\subsection{Algebraic Morse theory}
\label{subsec:algebraic-morse}

Roughly speaking, for a complex $(X_\bullet, d)$ that comes with a special gadget $\mathcal{M}$ called Morse matching, algebraic Morse theory says that we can obtain a `smaller' complex $(X_\bullet^{\mathcal M}, d^{\mathcal M})$ that is homotopic to $(X,d)$ by identifying terms appearing in a Morse matching appropriately. This usage of algebraic Morse theory we use follows \cite[Section~3]{CLZ26}.

\begin{dfn}\label{dfn:morse-matching}
Let $(X_\bullet,d)$ be a complex of right $A$-modules with fixed decompositions $X_n=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in I_n}X_{n,\alpha}$. Write $d_{\beta,\alpha}\colon X_{n,\alpha}\to X_{n-1,\beta}$ for the matrix components of $d_n$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The \defn{weighted quiver} $Q(X_\bullet)$ has the summands $X_{n,\alpha}$ as vertices and an arrow $X_{n,\alpha}\xrightarrow{d_{\beta,\alpha}}X_{n-1,\beta}$ weighted by $d_{\beta,\alpha}$ whenever $d_{\beta,\alpha}\neq0$.

\item A \defn{partial matching} $\mathcal M$ is a collection of arrows such that
\begin{enumerate}
\item no two selected arrows share a vertex;
\item the weight of every selected arrow is an isomorphism.
\end{enumerate}

\item Form a new weighted quiver $Q^{\mathcal M}$ by
%\begin{enumerate}
%\item \bhx{retaining every unmatched arrow and drawing it as a \defn{thick} arrow;} \comment{I question what is the point of this terminology... just `unmatched' arrow is fine???}
%\item
replacing each matched arrow $W\xrightarrow{w}U$ by a \defn{dotted} arrow $U\to W$ in reverse direction of weight $-w^{-1}$.
%\end{enumerate}
A vertex not incident with a matched arrow is called \defn{critical}.

\item A directed path in $Q^{\mathcal M}$ is a \defn{zigzag path} if thick and dotted arrows alternate; a path consisting of one arrow is also allowed.  We shall use the following single path notation.  For a vertex $U$ in degree $n$ and a vertex $V$ in degree $n-1$, let $\Gamma_n^{\mathcal M}(U,V)$ be the set of zigzag paths from $U$ to $V$ of the form
\[
 U=U_0\morsethickarrow U_1
 \morsedottedarrow U_2
 \morsethickarrow\cdots
 \morsedottedarrow U_{2r}
 \morsethickarrow U_{2r+1}=V,
 \qquad r\geq0.
\]
The weight $\weight(\gamma)$ of a zigzag path $\gamma$ is the composition, along the order of the zigzag path $\gamma$, of the weights of its arrows.

\item The partial matching $\mathcal M$ is a \defn{Morse matching} if it satisfies the following local finiteness hypothesis: for every matched arrow $W\xrightarrow{w}U$, every vertex $V$ in the same degree, say, $n-1$ as $U$, and every $x\in U$, the sum
\[
 \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_n^{\mathcal M}(W,V)}
 \weight(\gamma)\bigl(-w^{-1}(x)\bigr)
\]
is well-defined, and it is non-zero for only finitely many vertices $V$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{dfn}

One special case where the the local finiteness hypothesis  can be conveniently verified is the following.

\begin{prop}\textnormal{(\cite[Proposition~3.2]{CLZ26})}
\label{prop:morse-sufficient-condition}
Let $\mathcal M$ be a partial matching of $Q(X_\bullet)$.  If $Q^{\mathcal M}$ contains no infinite zigzag path, then $\mathcal M$ is a Morse matching.
\end{prop}

We next record the main result of algebraic Morse theory.

\begin{thm}\textnormal{(\cite[Theorem~3.3]{CLZ26})}
\label{thm:algebraic-morse}
Let $\mathcal M$ be a Morse matching of $Q(X_\bullet)$, and write $\Crit_n(\mathcal M)$ for its critical vertices in degree $n$.  Then we have complex $(X_\bullet^{\mathcal{M}},d^{\mathcal{M}})$, called \defn{Morse complex}, given by
\[
 X_n^{\mathcal M}
 :=\bigoplus_{U\in\Crit_n(\mathcal M)}U,  \qquad d_{V,U}^{\mathcal M}
 :=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_n^{\mathcal M}(U,V)} \weight(\gamma),
\]
where $d^{\mathcal{M}}_{V,U}$ is the $(V,U)$-component of $d_n^{\mathcal{M}}:X_n^{\mathcal{M}}\to X_{n-1}^{\mathcal{M}}$ for $U\in\Crit_n(\mathcal M)$ and $V\in\Crit_{n-1}(\mathcal M)$.
%For critical vertices $U$ in degree $n$ and $V$ in degree $n-1$, define the $(V,U)$-component of $d_n^{\mathcal M}\colon X_n^{\mathcal M}\to X_{n-1}^{\mathcal M}$ by
%\[
% d_{V,U}^{\mathcal M}
% :=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\mathcal M}(U,V)}
% \weight(\gamma).
%\]
%Then $(X_\bullet^{\mathcal M},d^{\mathcal M})$ is a complex.  
Moreover, it is homotopy equivalent to $(X_\bullet,d)$ -- more precisely, one has a decomposition of chain complexes
\[
 (X_\bullet,d)\cong
 (X_\bullet^{\mathcal M},d^{\mathcal M})
 \oplus(Y_\bullet,d^Y),
\]
where $Y_\bullet$ is null-homotopic.
\end{thm}

In particular, if $X_\bullet$ is a projective resolution of a module, then its Morse complex is also a projective resolution of the same module.

\subsection{Construction of the Morse matching}
\label{subsec:construction-morse-matching}

Let us first explain the idea of how to find a Morse matching. The differential formula~\eqref{eq:candidate-differential} separates the faces of a strict chain into two types: the zeroth face deletes its initial member, whereas the remaining faces preserve the initial member.  Under the identification~\eqref{eq:candidate-terms}, these two contributions give a decomposition $d=\delta+\partial$, where the non-zero components of $\partial$ are signed identity maps between copies of the same projective, while those of $\delta$ is induced by the left-multiplication map (more precise formulation will come after the next paragraph).

The difficulty of finding a Morse matching is that the signed identity components of $\partial$ need not themselves form a partial matching, since several of them may share a source or target (see Example \ref{subsec:eg2}). The `solution' is to do what usual homological perturbation theory does: decompose using a choice of kernel--image splitting for each chain complex $C_\bullet(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}})$. With respect to this new choice of decomposition, the matrix components of both $\partial$ and $\delta$ will change, but $\partial$ becomes a collection of isomorphisms with pairwise disjoint endpoints, whereas every matrix component arising from $\delta$ are still radical maps.  The former components will define the Morse matching, and the latter property will control its zigzag paths.

\medskip

We now construct the matching. All tensor products below are over $\Bbbk$. For $Z\in\mathcal J(P)$, put
\[
 C_{Z,\bullet}:=\widetilde C_\bullet(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}})[1],
 \qquad C_{Z,k}=\widetilde C_{k-1}(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}}),
 \qquad \partial^Z_k:C_{Z,k}\longrightarrow C_{Z,k-1},
\]
where $\partial^Z_k = -\partial_{k-1}$ is the negative of the boundary map on $\widetilde{C}_\bullet(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}})$. Thus $C_{Z,0}=\Bbbk 1_Z$, $C_{Z,k}=0$ for $k<0$, and $\partial^Z_0=0$.

\]
Note that here we have $\underline{Z}<\underline{Z}_1<\cdots<\underline{Z}_k$; when $T=Z_1$, the output is a basis element of $C_{T,k-1}$. 
Under \eqref{eq:candidate-terms},  we have, for $k\geq1$,

\begin{thm}\label{thm:minimal-resolution}

 \cdots\longrightarrow\mathcal P_{\min,2}
 \longrightarrow\mathcal P_{\min,1}

\]

 \end{aligned}

\end{equation}
where $\widetilde\beta_{Z,k}:= \dim_{\Bbbk}\widetilde H_{k-1}(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}})$.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof} 
To see that $\mathcal M$ is a partial matching, notice first that both the source $P_Z\otimes L_{Z,k}$ and the target $P_Z\otimes B_{Z,k-1}$ of each displayed map have label $Z$. Since every nonzero component of $\delta_k$ changes the label from $Z$ to some $T$ with $\underline{Z}<\underline{T}$, the component of $\delta_k$ between these two summands is zero. Hence the component of $d_k=\partial_k+\delta_k$ from $P_Z\otimes L_{Z,k}$ to $P_Z\otimes B_{Z,k-1}$ is exactly $\id_{P_Z}\otimes\left.\partial^Z_k\right|_{L_{Z,k}}$. Their endpoints are pairwise disjoint and their weights are isomorphisms, so $\mathcal M$ is a partial matching.

To show that $\mathcal M$ is a Morse matching, it follows from Proposition~\ref{prop:morse-sufficient-condition} that showing $\mathcal M$ has no infinite zigzag path will suffice.
Indeed, since every nonzero component of $\partial$ belongs to $\mathcal M$, every unmatched arrow in $Q^{\mathcal M}$ is a component of $\delta$. Thus, for $k\geq1$, every unmatched arrow has the form
\[
 P_Z\otimes E_{Z,k}
 \xrightarrow{\mu_{T,Z}\otimes\delta'_{T,Z}}
 P_T\otimes F_{T,k-1}
 \quad \text{ with }\underline{Z}<\underline{T},
\]
for appropriate $E,F\in\{B,H,L\}$ and the map $\delta'_{T,Z}$ is given by the composition
\begin{equation*}\label{eq:delta'}
 \delta'_{T,Z}:
 E_{Z,k}\hookrightarrow C_{Z,k}
 \xrightarrow{\delta_{T,Z,k}}C_{T,k-1}
 \twoheadrightarrow F_{T,k-1}.
\end{equation*}
On the other hand, every dotted arrow goes from $P_Z\otimes B_{Z,k-1}$ to $P_Z\otimes L_{Z,k}$. Hence, along a zigzag path, dotted arrows preserve the label $\underline{Z}\in\mathcal D_X$, whereas unmatched arrows strictly
increase it. Since $\mathcal D_X$ is finite and the two types of arrows alternate, zigzag paths have bounded length. In particular, there is no infinite zigzag path, as required.

Theorem~\ref{thm:algebraic-morse} yields a Morse complex $(\mathcal P_{\min,\bullet},d_{\min})$ homotopy equivalent to $\mathcal P_\bullet$.
Its terms are projective, and $\mathcal P_\bullet$ resolves $I_X$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:exactness}.  Hence $\mathcal P_{\min,\bullet}$ is a projective resolution of $I_X$.  To see that the individual terms are as described in \eqref{eq:minimal-terms}, recall from our choice of decomposition \eqref{eq:homology-splitting} and $\mathcal{M}$ that every nonzero summand $P_Z\otimes B_{Z,k}$ is matched with $P_Z\otimes L_{Z,k+1}$.
Thus the critical summands in degree $k$ are precisely the nonzero $P_Z\otimes H_{Z,k}$. Together with $H_{Z,k}\cong\widetilde H_{k-1}(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}})$ yields the required description \eqref{eq:minimal-terms}. 

It remains to prove minimality. Note that every unmatched arrow has weight $\mu_{T,Z}\otimes\delta'_{T,Z}:P_Z\otimes E_{Z,k}\to P_T\otimes F_{T,k-1}$, where $k\geq1$ and $\underline{Z}<\underline{T}$, with appropriate $E,F\in\{B,H,L\}$.
Since $Z\neq T$, we have $b_{TZ}\in e_T\rad\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)}$, which implies that the map $\mu_{T,Z}$ given by left-multiplying $b_{TZ}$ is radical.  Hence, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:image muTZ otimes delta'}    
 \Image(\mu_{T,Z}\otimes\delta'_{T,Z})
 \subseteq (P_T\rad\Sigma_{\mathcal J(P)})\otimes F_{T,k-1}
 = \rad P_T\otimes F_{T,k-1}.
\end{equation}
Fix $k\geq1$ and a zigzag path $\gamma$ from $P_Z\otimes H_{Z,k}$ to $P_T\otimes H_{T,k-1}$ contributing to $d_{\min,k}$. Since the target of $\gamma$ is critical, the last arrow of $\gamma$ has to be unmatched. 
The image of $\weight(\gamma)$ is contained in the image of this last arrow, so the inclusion \eqref{eq:image muTZ otimes delta'} applied to $F=H$ gives $\Image\weight(\gamma)\subseteq\rad P_T\otimes H_{T,k-1}$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:algebraic-morse}, each matrix component of $d_{\min,k}$ is a sum of these path weights; the sum is finite because $Q^{\mathcal M}$ is finite and its zigzag paths have bounded length. Hence $\Image d_{\min,k}\subseteq\rad\mathcal P_{\min,k-1}$ for every $k\geq1$, which proves minimality.
\end{proof}

The choices in \eqref{eq:homology-splitting} may change the individual differentials in the Morse complex.  Nevertheless, the resulting complexes are minimal projective resolutions of the same module and are therefore isomorphic.  In particular, $P_Z$ occurs in degree $k$ precisely when $\widetilde H_{k-1}(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}})\neq0$.

\begin{rmk}
Note that the multiplicity of $P_Z$ appearing in the minimal projective resolution of $I_X$ is given by $\dim_\Bbbk\Ext_A^k(I_X,S_Z)$, and so we have 
\[\widetilde{\beta}_{Z,k}=\dim_{\Bbbk}\widetilde H_{k-1}(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}})=\dim_{\Bbbk}\Ext_A^k(I_X,S_Z).
 \]
\end{rmk}

We can now refine Corollary \ref{cor:distributive-ig} when working over a field.
\begin{cor}\label{cor:SigmaJ(P) is IG} Assume that the ground ring is a field $R=\Bbbk$ and $P\neq\emptyset$.
For every $X\in\mathcal J(P)$, the indecomposable injective module $I_X$ over the distance $\Bbbk$-algebra $\Sigma_L$ satisfies
\begin{align}
 \pdim_{\Sigma_L} I_X
 &=\max\left\{k\geq0\ \middle|\
 \widetilde H_{k-1}(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}})\neq0
 \text{ for some }Z\in\mathcal J(P)\right\}
 \label{eq:projective-dimension}
\end{align}
In particular, for every $Z\in\mathcal J(P)$, $ \widetilde\beta_{Z,k} =\dim_{\Bbbk}\widetilde H_{k-1}(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}})=0$ if any of the following is satisfied
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $k=|P|$.
    \item $k=|P|-1$ and $|P|\ge 3$. 
\end{enumerate}
\end{cor}
\begin{proof}
The projective dimension formula follows from the minimal terms in \eqref{eq:minimal-terms}. 
The Betti number vansihing follows from combining Corollary~\ref{cor:distributive-ig} and Proposition~\ref{prop:n-2-inequalities} with \eqref{eq:projective-dimension}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Projective dimension and Castelnuovo--Mumford regularity}
The formula for $\pdim I_X$ bears a striking resemblance to the characterisation of Castelnuovo--Mumford regularity by reduced homology:
\begin{align}
\pdim I_X
&=\max\{k\geq 0:\widetilde H_{k-1}(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}})\neq 0 \text{ for some }Z\in L\}, \\
\operatorname{reg}\Bbbk[K]
&=\max\{k\geq 0:\widetilde H_{k-1}(\operatorname{lk}_K F)\neq 0 \text{ for some }F\in K\}. \label{eq:reg=non-vanishing homology}
\end{align}
Here $K$ is a finite simplicial complex, $\Bbbk[K]$ is its Stanley--Reisner ring and $\operatorname{reg}$ denotes the Castelnuovo--Mumford regularity (Definition \ref{def:SR ring}), and $\operatorname{lk}_K F$ is the link of $F$ in $K$ (Definition \ref{def:link,face poset,sd}). For the equality \eqref{eq:reg=non-vanishing homology}, it is an application (Lemma \ref{lem:Hochshster easier}) of a more well-known Hochster's formula. This motivates us to seek a complex $K$ for which we can associate to each $\Delta^{>\underline{Z}}$ a homologous link $\operatorname{lk}_K F$.

We explain the idea of how to seek a suitable $K$. First, the most natural choice of $K$ should be a refinement of $\D_X$ since we hope every $\Delta^{>\underline{Z}}$ can appear as a link (or at least homologous to it), and it seems to be the nicest case is to have $\underline{Z}$ as a face. Next, if we approach from the link side, then it seems natural to replace the link in \eqref{eq:reg=non-vanishing homology} by the order complex of its non-empty-face poset $\mathcal F^\times(\operatorname{lk}_K F)$, so that we get ourselves to align as close as possible to $\Delta^{>\underline{Z}}$. More precisely, Lemma~\ref{lem:munkres} gives $\widetilde H_k(\Delta(\mathcal F^\times(\operatorname{lk}_K F)))\cong\widetilde H_k(\operatorname{lk}_K F)$ for all $k$.

Now that we have order complexes of two different posets, we can then use order homotopy thoerem \cite[Theorem 10.11]{Bjo95} to see if their order complexes have the same reduced homology by just finding order-preserving maps. Since we are looking at faces (i.e. sets) containing $F$, and we want $F=\underline{Z}$, and we also want an order-preserving map from $(\underline{Z},\hat{1}]$ to a face poset, these lead us to the following choice of $K$:
\[
\mathcal S_X:=\{S\subseteq P \mid S\subseteq \underline{T} \text{ for some }\underline{T}\in\mathcal D_X\} = \bigcup_{\underline{T}\in \max \D_X} 2^{\underline{T}},
\]
Indeed, a face in $\mathcal{S}_X$ containing some $\underline{Z}$ tells us possible choices to extend $\underline{Z}$ to a chain, which feels very close to what $\Delta^{>\underline{Z}}$ describes. Our main result of this subsection is the following.

\begin{thm}\label{thm:pdim=reg}
Let $\Bbbk$ be a field, $\Sigma_{\mathcal{J}(P)}$ be the distance algebra associated to the lattice $\mathcal{J}(P)$ defined over $\Bbbk$. Then, for any $X\in\mathcal{J}(P)$, we have
\[
\operatorname{pdim}_A I_X=\operatorname{reg}\Bbbk[\mathcal S_X].
\]
\end{thm}

In fact, the first step of the proof is to show that $\widetilde H_r(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}})\cong\widetilde H_r(\operatorname{lk}_{\mathcal S_X}\underline{Z})$ for all $r\ge -1$, which gives us $\pdim I_X\le \operatorname{reg} \Bbbk[\mathcal{S}_X]$. To get the equality, the second step is to show that $\operatorname{lk}_{\mathcal S_X}F$ has zero reduced homology whenever $F\in\mathcal S_X\setminus\mathcal D_X$. Both steps use the fact that $\mathcal D_X$ is closed under intersection.

We use the topological preliminaries from Subsection~\ref{subsec:topological-tools} to compare $\D_X$ and $\mathcal S_X$.

\begin{lem}\label{lem:face closure map}
The poset $\mathcal D_X$ is closed under intersections of non-empty finite families. The map
\[
c:\mathcal F(\mathcal S_X)\longrightarrow\mathcal D_X,\qquad
F\longmapsto\bigcap_{\substack{\underline{Y}\in\max\mathcal D_X\\F\subseteq \underline{Y}}}\underline{Y}
\]
is well-defined, preserves inclusion, and satisfies $F\subseteq c(F)$.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
For $Y,Z\in\mathcal{J}(P)$, the we have an order ideal $\nabla^{Y,Z}_X$ so that $\underline{\nabla^{Y,Z}_X} = \underline{Y}\cap \underline{Z}$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:symmetric-difference-identities}\textup{(\ref{itm:symdiff-intersection})}. Hence, closure under intersections of non-empty finite families follows by induction.

Every $F\in\mathcal S_X$ is contained in a facet, so the family defining $c(F)$ is non-empty. Closure under intersections gives $c(F)\in\mathcal D_X$, and every set in the intersection contains $F$. If $F\subseteq G$, the family of facets containing $G$ is contained in the family containing $F$. Therefore $c(F)\subseteq c(G)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lem}\label{lem:H(Delta Z)=H(lk DZ)}
For every $Z\in\mathcal J(P)$ and every $k\geq-1$,
\[
\widetilde H_k(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}})\cong
\widetilde H_k\bigl(\operatorname{lk}_{\mathcal S_X}(\underline{Z})\bigr).
\]
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
The set $\underline{Z}$ is a face of $\mathcal S_X$. Consider the order-preserving maps
\[
j_Z:(\underline{Z},\hat{1}]\longrightarrow\mathcal F(\mathcal S_X)_{>\underline{Z}},
\qquad \underline{T}\longmapsto \underline{T},
\]
\[
c_Z:\mathcal F(\mathcal S_X)_{>\underline{Z}}\longrightarrow(\underline{Z},\hat{1}],
\qquad F\longmapsto c(F).
\]
The map $c_Z$ is well-defined because $\underline{Z}<F\subseteq c(F)$ by Lemma \ref{lem:face closure map}. Moreover, we have
\[
\underline{T}\subset c_Zj_Z(\underline{T}), \text{ for all $\underline{T}\in(\underline{Z},\hat{1}]$ and }
F\subseteq  j_Zc_Z \text{ for all $F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{S}_X)_{>\underline{Z}}$.}
\]
Hence, Lemma \ref{lem:order homotopy} says that $j_{Z,\#}$ and $c_{Z,\#}$ are chain homotopy inverses. Thus, the order complexes of $(\underline{Z},\hat{1}]$ and $\mathcal F(\mathcal S_X)_{>\underline{Z}}$ have isomorphic reduced homology. Now applies Lemma \ref{lem:munkres} and get that
\[
\widetilde{H}_k(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}}) 
\cong \widetilde{H}_k(\Delta\big(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{S}_X)_{\underline{Z}}\big)) 
\cong \widetilde{H}_k(\operatorname{lk}_{\mathcal{S}_X}{\underline{Z}}) \text{ for all }k\ge -1,
\]
which proves the claim.
\end{proof}

Now we need to look at the reduce homologies of links of faces $F\in \mathcal{S}_X\setminus \D_X$.

\begin{lem}\label{lem:bad face has 0 homology link}
If $F\in\mathcal S_X$ and $F\notin\mathcal D_X$, then $\operatorname{lk}_{\mathcal S_X}(F)$ is a cone. In particular,
\[
\widetilde H_i\bigl(\operatorname{lk}_{\mathcal S_X}(F)\bigr)=0
\qquad\text{for every }i.
\]
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
Since $c(F)\in\mathcal D_X$ and $F\notin\mathcal D_X$, the inclusion $F\subseteq c(F)$ is strict. Choose $p\in c(F)\setminus F$.

For $G\in\operatorname{lk}_{\mathcal S_X}(F)$, choose a facet $\underline{Y}$ containing $F\cup G$. The definition of $c(F)$ gives $p\in \underline{Y}$, so $F\cup G\cup\{p\}\subseteq \underline{Y}$. Since $p\notin F$, it follows that $G\cup\{p\}\in\operatorname{lk}_{\mathcal S_X}(F)$. Thus the link is a cone with vertex $p$.
\end{proof}

Let us review the necessary background we need about Stanley--Reisner ring before proving Theorem \ref{thm:pdim=reg}.

\begin{dfn}\label{def:SR ring}
Let $K$ be a finite simplicial complex and put $S_K:=\Bbbk[x_v\mid v\in V(K)]$, with $\deg x_v=1$. Its \emph{Stanley--Reisner ideal} and \emph{Stanley--Reisner ring} are
\[
I(K):=\left(\prod_{v\in F}x_v\ \middle|\ F\subseteq V(K),\ F\notin K\right),
\qquad \Bbbk[K]:=S_K/I(K).
\]
Thus a product of distinct variables is non-zero in $\Bbbk[K]$ precisely when its support is a face of $K$.

Write the $i$-th term of the minimal graded free resolution of $\Bbbk[K]$ over $S_K$ as $\bigoplus_j S_K(-j)^{\oplus \beta_{i,j}}$, where $(-j)$ denotes the grading shift so that $1\in S_K(-j)$ is in degree $j$. The \emph{Castelnuovo--Mumford regularity} is
\[
\operatorname{reg}\Bbbk[K]:=\max\{j-i\mid\beta_{i,j}\neq0\}.
\]
For $K=\{\emptyset\}$, set $S_K=\Bbbk$, so $\operatorname{reg}\Bbbk[K]=0$.
\end{dfn}


\begin{lem}\label{lem:Hochshster easier}
For every finite simplicial complex $K$,
\[
\operatorname{reg}\Bbbk[K]
=\max\{k\geq0\mid\widetilde H_{k-1}(\operatorname{lk}_K(F))\neq0
\text{ for some }F\in K\}.
\]
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
This is a reformulation of Hochster's formula and interpretation of Castelnuovo--Mumford regularity using local cohomology; we clarify the reason for completeness and refer unexplained terminologies and references to \cite[Section A.7]{HH11}.  Let $S:=S_K$ and $M:=S_K/I(K)$.
Consider the local cohomology $H^i_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)$, which is a $\ZZ^{V(K)}$-graded module.
Now we have equality
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{reg}(M) 
&=\max\{i+j\mid H^i_{\mathfrak m}(M)_j\neq 0, \ j\in \ZZ\}\\
&=\max \{ i+\sum_v \alpha_v \mid H^i_{\mathfrak m}(M)_\alpha \neq 0, \ \alpha\in \ZZ^{V(K)}\}\\
&=\max\{i-|F| \,:\, \widetilde H_{i-|F|-1}(\operatorname{lk}_K F)\neq0,\ F\in K\},
\end{align*}
where the first equality is the local cohomology reformulation, the second follows by un-collapsing the total $\ZZ$-grading to the $\ZZ^{V(K)}$-grading, and the last equality follows from Hochster's formula \cite[Theorem A.7.3]{HH11}.
Note that $\widetilde{H}_{i-|F|-1}(\operatorname{lk}_KF)\neq 0$ implies that $i-|F|-1\ge -1$, and so $i-|F|$ is always non-negative, and we get the stated formula.
\end{proof}


\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem \ref{thm:pdim=reg}]
This follows from
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{pdim}_A I_X
&=\max\{k\geq0\mid\widetilde H_{k-1}(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}})\neq0
             \text{ for some }Z\in\mathcal J(P)\}\\
&=\max\{k\geq0\mid\widetilde H_{k-1}(\operatorname{lk}_{\mathcal S_X}(F))\neq0
             \text{ for some }F\in\mathcal S_X\}\\
&=\operatorname{reg}\Bbbk[\mathcal S_X],
\end{align*}
where the first equality follows from Corollary \ref{cor:SigmaJ(P) is IG} and the last equality follows from Lemma \ref{lem:Hochshster easier}. For the middle equality, we get $\le$ from Lemma \ref{lem:H(Delta Z)=H(lk DZ)}, whereas $\ge$ follows from Lemma \ref{lem:bad face has 0 homology link}.
\end{proof}

\section{Multiplicity in low degrees}
\label{sec:low-degree-multiplicity}

We now investigate further properties of the minimal projective resolution constructed above. \old{Recall that for a finitely generated projective right $A$-module $Q$, by the Krull--Schmidt theorem, it has a decomposition
\[
 Q\cong\bigoplus_{Z\in\mathcal J(P)}P_Z^{\oplus m_Z}.
\]
The integer $m_Z$ is the \defn{multiplicity}\comment{terminology already used before this point...} of $P_Z$ in $Q$, denoted $\bigl[Q:P_Z\bigr]$.  We call $Q$ \defn{multiplicity-free} if $\bigl[Q:P_Z\bigr]\leq1$ for every $Z\in\mathcal J(P)$.} \xnew{moved to Section 2}

For the minimal resolution of $I_X$ constructed in Theorem~\ref{thm:minimal-resolution}, equation~\eqref{eq:minimal-terms} gives
\begin{equation*}
 \bigl[\mathcal P_{\min,k}:P_Z\bigr]
 =\widetilde\beta_{Z,k}
 =\dim_{\Bbbk}\widetilde H_{k-1}(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}}).
\end{equation*}
In the following subsections, we see some possible behavior exhibit by these multiplicities.

\subsection{Multiplicity-free in degree zero and one}
\label{subsec:multi-free deg 0 1}
In degrees zero and one, the topology of $\Delta^{>\underline{Z}}$ is sufficiently restricted to give the following multiplicity-free results.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:low-degree-multiplicity}
The projective modules $\mathcal P_{\min,0}$ and $\mathcal P_{\min,1}$ are multiplicity-free.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
For $k=0$, the multiplicity of $P_Z$ is $\dim_{\Bbbk}\widetilde H_{-1}(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}})$, which is $1$ when $\Delta^{>\underline{Z}}=\emptyset$ and is $0$ otherwise.

Now consider $k=1$.  We have
\begin{equation*}
 \widetilde\beta_{Z,1}
 =\dim_{\Bbbk}\widetilde H_0(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}})
 =\#\{\text{connected components of the $1$-skeleton of }\Delta^{>\underline{Z}}\}-1.
\end{equation*}
The $1$-skeleton of $\Delta^{>\underline{Z}}$ is a graph whose  elements are $\underline{Y}\in\D_X^{>\underline{Z}}$, and two vertices $\underline{Y},\underline{Y'}$ are adjacent precisely when they are comparable. It then suffices to show that the $1$-skeleton of $\Delta^{>\underline{Z}}$ has at most two connected components. Note that if $\D_X^{>\underline{Z}}=\emptyset$, then the $1$-skeleton of $\Delta^{>\underline{Z}}$ is just the point $\emptyset$ and $\widetilde H_0(\Delta^{>\underline{Z}})=0$.

Every vertex $\underline{Y}\in \D_X^{>\underline{Z}}$ falls into one of the three cases (a) $Y\subsetneq Z$, (b) $Z\subsetneq Y$, or (c) $Y$ and $Z$ are incomparable.  In case (a), if $Y,Y'\subsetneq Z$, then $Y\cap Y'$ is an order ideal. Lemma~\ref{lem:symmetric-difference-identities}\textup{(\ref{itm:symdiff-inclusion})} gives $Z\setminus Y,Z\setminus Y'\subseteq X$. Similarly applying Lemma~\ref{lem:symmetric-difference-identities}\textup{(\ref{itm:symdiff-inclusion})} to $Y,Y\cap Y'$ and to $Y',Y\cap Y'$ yields
\[
 \underline{Y}\subseteq \underline{Y\cap Y'},\qquad
 \underline{Y'}\subseteq \underline{Y\cap Y'},\qquad
 \underline{Z}\subsetneq \underline{Y\cap Y'}.
\]
Thus all vertices in case (a) lie in one connected component.

For case (b), given $Z\subsetneq Y,Y'$, then $Y\cup Y'$ is an order ideal. By Lemma~\ref{lem:symmetric-difference-identities}\textup{(\ref{itm:symdiff-inclusion})}, both $Y\setminus Z$ and $Y'\setminus Z$ are disjoint from $X$. Applying the same item to $Y,Y\cup Y'$ and to $Y',Y\cup Y'$ yields
\[
 \underline{Y}\subseteq \underline{Y\cup Y'},\qquad
 \underline{Y'}\subseteq \underline{Y\cup Y'},\qquad
 \underline{Z}\subsetneq \underline{Y\cup Y'}.
\]
Thus all vertices in case (b) lie in one connected component.

Finally, suppose that $Y$ and $Z$ are incomparable. Since $\underline{Z}\subsetneq\underline{Y}$, Lemma~\ref{lem:symmetric-difference-identities}\textup{(\ref{itm:symdiff-interval})} gives
\[
 \underline{Y\cap Z}=\underline{Z}\sqcup(Z\setminus Y),\qquad
 \underline{Y\cup Z}=\underline{Z}\sqcup(Y\setminus Z).
\]
Both are vertices of the $1$-skeleton and are comparable with $\underline{Y}$; the first corresponds to an order ideal properly contained in $Z$, whereas the second corresponds to one properly containing $Z$.  Consequently, any $Y$ incomparable with $Z$ belongs to one of the two components described.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Higher multiplicities via independence complexes}
\label{subsec:higher-multiplicities}

In contrast to the behavior in degree $0$ and $1$, we demonstrate that multiplicities can be arbitrary large in higher degrees already when $P$ is a height-two poset.

Let $B=(X\sqcup Y,E)$ be a (finite) bipartite graph so we can regard $E\subseteq X\times Y$.  Specifying such a $B$ is equivalent to specifying a height-two poset, namely, the corresponding poset is given by $(P_B=X\sqcup Y, \le)$ with
\[
 x<y
 \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad
 \{x,y\}\in E
 \qquad(x\in X,\ y\in Y),
\]
and no other strict relations.  Conversely, the Hasse diagram of a height two poset $(P=X\sqcup Y, \le)$, where $X$ is of height $0$ and $Y$ is of height $1$, is precisely the bipartite graph $B$ with $P_B=P$.

A subset $\sigma\subseteq V(B)$ is \defn{independent} if no two vertices of $\sigma$ are joined by an edge of $B$.  The \defn{independence complex} $\Ind(B)$ is the simplicial complex on $V(B)$ whose faces are the independent subsets of $B$.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:height-two-independence}
For a bipartite graph $B=(X\sqcup Y,E)$, the poset $\mathcal F\bigl(\Ind(B)\bigr)$ is the same as $\D_X$ associated to the height-two poset $P_B$. In particular, we have $\sd\Ind(B)=\Delta(\D_X\setminus\{\emptyset\}) = \Delta^{>\underline{X}}$, and hence
\[
 [\mathcal P_{\min,k}:P_X] = \beta_{X,k} = \widetilde H_{k-1}(\Delta^{>\underline{X}})
 \cong\widetilde H_{k-1}(\Ind(B))
 \text{ for all } k\ge 0.
\]
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Fix $T\in \mathcal{J}(P_B)$.  We show that $\underline{T}$ is an independent set of $B$.  Indeed, note that $\underline{T}=(X\setminus T)\sqcup(T\cap Y)$, so if, on the contrary, $x\in X\setminus T$ and $y\in T\cap Y$ were adjacent in $B$, then $x<y$ in $P_B$.  But $T$ being an order ideal implies that $x\in T$, a contradiction.

Conversely, let $U$ be an independent set of $B$, we claim that $T_U:=\bigl(X\setminus(U\cap X)\bigr)\cup(U\cap Y)\in \mathcal{J}(P_B)$.  Indeed, if $y\in U\cap y$ and $x<y$ in $P_B$, then independence gives $x\notin U$, so $x\in T_U$. Since $T_U=X\SDiff U$, Lemma~\ref{lem:symmetric-difference-identities}\textup{(\ref{itm:symdiff-cancellation})} gives
\[
 \underline{T_U}=X\SDiff(X\SDiff U)=U.
\]
Hence we get that $\mathcal F(\Ind(B))$ are the same collection of subsets as $\D_X$.  Since both posets use inclusion order, they are the same poset.

The last assertion follows from $\underline{X}=\emptyset$, which holds by Lemma~\ref{lem:symmetric-difference-identities}\textup{(\ref{itm:symdiff-associativity})}.
\end{proof}

By Proposition~\ref{prop:height-two-independence}, a bipartite graph $B$ with $\Ind(B)$ that has reduced homology of dimension greater than one gives a non-multiplicity-free term we desired.

\section{A simplicial construction for thin modules}
\label{app:thin-modules}

The projective resolution in Section~\ref{sec:candidate-resolution} is an instance of the simplicial replacement (bar construction of \cite[Definition 4.2.1]{Rie14}) of a diagram of projective modules.  This explains why it looks very much like an economoical reformulation of bar construction.  For some modules, this construction always gives a complex of projectives, although the complex need not be exact.

Let $A=\Bbbk Q/I$ be a basic finite-dimensional bound-quiver algebra, and let $M$ be a \defn{thin} right $A$-module, i.e. $\dim_{\Bbbk}Me_x\leq1$ for every $i\in Q_0$.  Let $Q_M$ be the subquiver of $Q$ whose vertices are
\[
(Q_M)_0:=\{x\in Q_0\mid Me_x\neq0\}
\]
and whose arrows are those $a:x\to y$ for which right multiplication induces a non-zero map $Me_x\to Me_y$.  The key assumption we need is that non-zero vectors $m_x\in Me_x$ can be chosen so that
\begin{equation}
m_x a=m_y
\qquad
\text{for every arrow $a:x\to y$ in $Q_M$}.
\label{eq:thin-normalized-basis}
\end{equation}

The quiver $Q_M$ is acyclic.  Indeed, if it contained an oriented cycle, then \eqref{eq:thin-normalized-basis} would imply that every positive power of this cycle acts non-trivially on one of the vectors $m_x$.  This contradicts the admissibility of $I$, since every sufficiently long path belongs to $I$.

For every $x\in(Q_M)_0$, let $\pi_x\colon P_x=e_xA\to M$ be the homomorphism determined by $\pi_x(e_x)=m_x$.  Since each non-zero space $Me_x$ is spanned by $m_x$, these maps give a surjection
\begin{equation}\label{eq: pi to M}
d_0:=\bigl(\pi_x\bigr)_{x\in(Q_M)_0}\colon
\bigoplus_{x\in(Q_M)_0}P_x\to M.
\end{equation}

Consider the small category $\mathcal C_M$ associated to $M$ given as follows.  We take $(Q_M)_0$ as the objects of $\mathcal{C}_M$. For $x,y\in(Q_M)_0$, define
\[
\mathcal C_M(y,x)
:= \left\{ \mu_p:P_y\to P_x \mid p:x\to y\text{ is a path on }Q_M \right\},
\]
where $\mu_p:P_y\to P_x$ is the homomorphism of projective $A$-modules given by left-multiplying $p$. In particular, two paths $p,p':x\to y$ determine the same morphism of $\mathcal C_M$ precisely when $\mu_p=\mu_{p'}$, and the identity of $x$ is $\mu_{e_x} = \id_{P_x}$. If there is no path between $x,y$ on $Q_M$, then we take $\mathcal{C}_M(y,x)=\emptyset$. Composition is given by $\mu_p\mu_q=\mu_{pq}$ whenever $p:x\to y$ and $q:y\to z$.

Define a functor
\begin{equation*}
F_M\colon\mathcal C_M\longrightarrow\proj A,
\qquad
i\longmapsto P_i,
\qquad
\mu_p\longmapsto\mu_p.
\end{equation*}
By \eqref{eq:thin-normalized-basis}, it follows that $\pi_x\mu_p=\pi_y$, so the homomorphisms $\pi_x$ form a cocone over $F_M$ with vertex $M$, which means that there is a surjection induced by $d_0$ of \eqref{eq: pi to M}
\[
\colim_{\mathcal{C}_M} F_M\cong  \Big(\bigoplus_{x\in \mathcal{C}_M} P_x \Big) / \sum_{\substack{p:x\to y\\ m\in P_x}} (m - mp) A \twoheadrightarrow M.
\]

For $k\geq0$, let $N_k(\mathcal C_M)$ be the set of composable strings of $k$ non-identity morphisms
\begin{equation*}
x_\ast=
\bigl(
x_0\xrightarrow{f_1}x_1\xrightarrow{f_2}\cdots
\xrightarrow{f_k}x_k
\bigr).
\end{equation*}
In particular, $N_0(\mathcal C_M)$ is the object set of $\mathcal C_M$.  For $0\leq r\leq k$, write $x_\ast^{\widehat r}$ for the string obtained by deleting $i_r$ and (only in the case of $r\neq 0,k$) composing the adjacent morphisms.  Define
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal P_k(M):=
\bigoplus_{x_\ast\in N_k(\mathcal C_M)}
F_M(i_0)^{(x_\ast)} = \bigoplus_{x_\ast\in N_k(\mathcal C_M)}
P_{i_0}^{(x_\ast)}.
\end{equation*}
Here \(F_M(i_0)^{(x_\ast)}\) denotes a labelled copy of \(F_M(i_0)=P_{i_0}\).  For $k>0$, define
\[
d_k\colon\mathcal P_k(M)\longrightarrow\mathcal P_{k-1}(M),
\qquad u^{(x_\ast)}
\mapsto
\bigl(F_M(f_1)(u)\bigr)^{(x_\ast^{\widehat0})}
+\sum_{r=1}^k(-1)^r
u^{(x_\ast^{\widehat r})}.
\]
%\end{equation*}
Thus the zeroth face changes the initial projective through $F_M(f_1)$, while every other face acts as the identity on the initial projective.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:cpx from module}
For a thin $A$-module $M$, the maps $d_k$ above yield a complex of $A$-modules
\begin{equation*}
\cdots\longrightarrow\mathcal P_2(M)
\xrightarrow{d_2}\mathcal P_1(M)
\xrightarrow{d_1}\mathcal P_0(M)
\xrightarrow{d_0}M\to 0,
\end{equation*}
which is exact at $\mathcal{P}_0(M)$ when $M$ is faithful.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
For a string $x_\ast$ and $0\leq r\leq k$, let $\varepsilon_r^{x_\ast}$ be the homomorphism defines analogous to \eqref{eq:face map}.  The zeroth face is induced by $F_M(f_1)$, while every other face is the identity map between the corresponding labelled copies of $F_M(i_0)$.  Functoriality of $F_M$ gives the simplicial identities
\begin{equation*}
\varepsilon_i^{x_\ast^{\widehat j}}
\varepsilon_j^{x_\ast}
=
\varepsilon_{j-1}^{x_\ast^{\widehat i}}
\varepsilon_i^{x_\ast}
\qquad
(0\leq i<j\leq k).
\end{equation*}
The only identity involving two non-identity homomorphisms is the one obtained by deleting $x_0$ and $x_1$, and it follows from $F_M(f_2)F_M(f_1)=F_M(f_2f_1)$.  For $0\leq i<j\leq k$, the equal composites $\varepsilon_i^{x_\ast^{\widehat j}}\varepsilon_j^{x_\ast}$ and $\varepsilon_{j-1}^{x_\ast^{\widehat i}}\varepsilon_i^{x_\ast}$ occur in $d_{k-1}d_k$ with coefficients $(-1)^{i+j}$ and $(-1)^{i+j-1}$, respectively. Hence $d_{k-1}d_k=0$ for $k\geq2$.

For a morphism $f:x_0\to x_1$ and $u\in P_{i_0}$, the the equality $\pi_{x_1}\circ F_M(f)=\pi_{x_0}$ gives
\[
d_0d_1\bigl(x^{(f)}\bigr)
=
\pi_{x_1}F_M(f)(u)-\pi_{x_0}(u)=0.
\]
Hence the displayed sequence is a complex.
\end{proof}

The set $N_k(\mathcal{C}_M)$ becomes the $k$-chains in $\Delta(\D_X)$.

\medskip

Let us explain the translation of $\mathcal M$ and $F$ now.
Recall that a simplicial right $A$-module is a functor $\Delta^{\mathrm{op}}\to\operatorname{Mod}A$, where $\Delta$ is the category of finite non-empty totally ordered sets $[n]=\{0<\ldots<n\}$ and order-preserving maps; roughly speaking, it is a sequence $\{M_k\}_{k\ge 0}$ of right $A$-modules (where \emph{degree $k$ term} $M_k$ is the functor evaluated at $[k]$) equipped with homomorphisms that delete or repeat positions, satisfying compatibility identities.  Every right $A$-module $N$ determines a \emph{constant simplicial module} $cN$, with $(cN)_k = N$ for all $k\ge 0$ where all structure maps are $\id_N$. We take
\[
\mathcal M=s(\operatorname{Mod}A),\qquad F=c\circ F_M:\mathcal C_M\longrightarrow s(\operatorname{Mod}A),
\]
where the inclusion $\operatorname{proj}A\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Mod}A$ is understood. Thus, we have $F(x)=cP_x$ for each $x\in \mathcal{C}_M=Q_M$.

Next, we unfold the definition of the simplicial bar construction to see how (the modules underlying) $\mathcal{P}_\bullet$ shows up in it. Writing out the evaluation of a string $\vec d:[k]\to \D$ yields a sequence $d_0\xrightarrow{f_1} d_1 \xrightarrow{f_2} \cdots \xrightarrow{f_k} d_k$.  These $d_j$'s are our $x_j\in Q_M$, and so with the chosen $F,G$ we have a summand
\[
Gd_k\otimes Fd_0 = \ast \otimes c(F_M(x_0)) \cong c F_M(x_0)
\]
indexed by $\vec d$ of $B_k(G,\D,F)$. Note that $\ast$ here is the terminal object in the category $\mathrm{sSet}$ of simplicial sets, that is the singleton set at every degree with all structure map being identity; taking the simplicial tensor with $\ast$ is naturally isomorphic to applying the identity functor, which explains the last isomorphism.

%Now the simplicial bar construction reads
%\[
%B_k(*,\mathcal C_M,cF_M)=\bigoplus_{x_\ast=(i_0\xrightarrow{f_1}\cdots\xrightarrow{f_k}i_k)} cF_M(i_0)
%\cong c\left(\bigoplus_{x_\ast=(i_0\xrightarrow{f_1}\cdots\xrightarrow{f_k}i_k)}F_M(i_0)\right).
%\]
%This is quite close to our formulation, but the sum includes strings where some of the $f_j$'s are identity.  Since the far-right is a constant simplicial module, we can identity this with the ordinary right $A$-module inside $c(-)$.

Next, we want to clarify the differential of the simplicial bar complex. For each fixed $k$, we have $k+1$ face maps $\partial_k^{(r)}:B_k(G,\D,F)\to B_{k-1}(G,\D,F)$ for $0\le r\le k$.   As we show in the table above, these are our $\varepsilon_r$ maps.  To see this, for $r=k$, its restriction to $Gd_k\otimes Fd_0$ is given by $G(f_k)\otimes\operatorname{id}_{F(d_0)}$, which translates to $ \epsilon_k^{(x_\ast)}:p^{(x_\ast)} \mapsto p^{(x_\ast^{\widehat k})} $ in our setting.  For $r=0$, the restriction to the same summand is
\[
\operatorname{id}_{G(d_k)}\otimes F(f_1):G(d_k)\otimes F(d_0)
\longrightarrow
G(d_k)\otimes F(d_1),
\]which is
$ \epsilon_0^{(x_\ast)}:p^{(x_\ast)}\mapsto (F_M(f_1)(p))^{(x_\ast^{\widehat 0})} $ in our setting. For $0<r<n$, $\partial^{(r)}$ composes $f_r$ and $f_{r+1}$, and leaves the element of $F(d_0)$ unchanged, which means that the same term-omitting map $\epsilon_r^{(x_\ast)}:p^{(x_\ast)} \mapsto p^{(x_\ast^{\widehat r})}$ in our setting. The differential of the (not-yet-normalized) simplicial bar complex $B_\bullet=B_\bullet(G,\D,F)$ is
\[
d_k^{\mathrm{un}}:B_k\longrightarrow B_{k-1},
\qquad
d_k^{\mathrm{un}} = \sum_{r=0}^{k}(-1)^r\partial_k^{(r)}.
\]
Note that $d_{k-1}^{\mathrm{un}}d_k^{\mathrm{un}}=0$ is just a consequence of $ \partial_{k-1}^{(r)}\partial_k^{(s)} = \partial_{k-1}^{(s-1)}\partial_k^{(r)}$ for any $0\le r<s\le k$; by the same argument we use in Proposition \ref{prop:cpx from module}.

To normalise the bar complex, we need to consider, for each $0\le r\le k-1$, the degeneracy map
\[
s_{k-1}^{(r)}:B_{k-1}\longrightarrow B_k,
\]
which inserts the identity morphism $\id_{i_r}$ into the string at the object $i_r$.  They satisfy
\begin{align}\label{eq:degeneracy identities}
\partial_k^{(r)}s_{k-1}^{(q)}
=
\begin{cases}
s_{k-2}^{(q-1)}\partial_{k-1}^{(r)},&r<q,\\
\id_{B_{k-1}},&r=q\text{ or }r=q+1,\\
s_{k-2}^{(q)}\partial_{k-1}^{(r-1)},&r>q+1.
\end{cases}\end{align}
Define
\[
C_k = \sum_{r=0}^{k-1}\operatorname{im}s_{k-1}^{(r)}
\subseteq B_k,
\text{ and } C_0=0.
\]
Then \eqref{eq:degeneracy identities} implies that $d_k^{\mathrm{un}}(C_k)\subseteq C_{k-1}$.  Consequently, we have a quotient complex $\overline{B}_\bullet$, called the \defn{normalized bar complex} given by
\[
\overline{B}_k:=B_k/C_k, \text{ with differential }d_k(b+C_k)=d_k^{\mathrm{un}}(b)+C_{k-1}.
\]

Since $\mathcal C_M$ has no non-identity isomorphisms, we have
\[
\overline{B}_k
\cong \bigoplus_{\substack{
x_0\xrightarrow{f_1}\cdots\xrightarrow{f_k}x_k\\
f_r\neq\id\ \text{for all }r
}} F_M(x_0) = \bigoplus_{f_* =(x_0 \to \cdots \to x_k ) \in N_k(\mathcal{C}_M)} F_M(x_0).
\]
Moreover, the differential is the alternating sum of the maps $\partial_k^{(r)}$ after deleting every term that contains an identity morphism, which implies that $\overline{B}_\bullet \cong \mathcal{P}_\bullet(M)$.

As a consequence of the identification with bar construction, we get that
\begin{equation*}
H_0\bigl(\mathcal P_\bullet(M)\bigr)
\cong\colim_{\mathcal C_M}F_M,
\qquad
H_k\bigl(\mathcal P_\bullet(M)\bigr)
\cong L_k\colim_{\mathcal C_M}F_M
\quad(k>0),
\end{equation*}
where $L_k$ denotes the $k$-th left-derived functor. In the general setting of $\mathcal{C}_M$, these higher derived colimits may not vanish and the colimit may not be the same $M$ itself, whereas Theorem~\ref{thm:exactness} proves that, in the case when $\mathcal{C}_M=\D_X$ (Example \ref{eg:DX is C_IX}), this will not be the case.
